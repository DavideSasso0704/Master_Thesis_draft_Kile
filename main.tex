\begin{center}
    {\Large\bfseries Abstract}
\end{center}

\vspace{1cm}
\noindent
The next generation of gravitational-wave (GW) observatories, Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA) and the Einstein Telescope (ET), will jointly probe compact objects with masses ranging from a few to $\sim 10^7\,M_\odot$ over the frequency interval extending from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{4}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. This unprecedented observational window makes it essential to identify promising GW sources, determine which of them may emit a detectable signal and characterize its physical properties. Among the most compelling candidates are intermediate-mass black hole (IMBH) binaries, whose inspiral GW emission would offer unique insights into this still poorly constrained population of massive black holes (MBHs).\\This thesis investigates the dynamical evolution of IMBH binaries embedded in stellar backgrounds, with particular emphasis on the  coalescence timescale and the time evolution of the binary separation and eccentricity. To this end, both the Quinlan--Sesana hybrid model and Ketju direct N-body simulations are employed. While the former approach provides an efficient framework for reconstructing the evolution of BH binaries once the stellar background properties and the binary initial conditions are specified, the Ketju module, implemented into GADGET-4, modeling SMBHs and stars as point particles during close encounters through an algorithmically regularized integrator, accurately captures the orbital decay driven by stellar scattering with unprecedented spatial resolution. The analysis explores different stellar backgrounds, including nucleated dwarf galaxies and observationally motivated high-redshift stellar clumps and galaxy models with enhanced stellar densities resulting from cosmic size evolution. The obtained results confirm that IMBH binaries embedded in nucleated dwarf galaxies can merge within a few Myr. More remarkably, they show that even non-nucleated high-redshift dwarf galaxies and stellar clumps may drive binary coalescence within a Hubble time, identifying these systems as promising sources of detectable gravitational waves. Finally, the reconstructed binary evolution is finally exploited to compute the associated GW signals, providing observational predictions for the LISA and Einstein Telescope era.

\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}


Massive black holes play a fundamental role in the formation and evolution of galaxies. Observations have established that they are ubiquitously found at the centers of nearby galaxies \parencite{Kormendy_1995}, while tight empirical correlations between their masses and the properties of the host galactic bulges suggest a close co-evolution of black holes and galaxies \parencite{Magorrian_1998}.
Moreover, MBHs are belived to be common in galaxy centres at all cosmic epochs; a piece of evidence for their existence, from low to high redshift, comes from the transient activity of galactic nuclei (AGN) over the hosts lifetime (see, e.g., \textcite{Jahnke_2025}).\\Black holes (BHs) span a wide range of masses $(\sim 10-10^{9}\,M_\odot)$ and various formation and accretion models have been proposed based on their mass. At the two extremes of the black-hole mass spectrum lie stellar-mass black holes and supermassive black holes (SMBHs). Stellar evolution theory predicts that stellar-mass BHs may be the remnants of massive stars with either Zero Age Main Sequence mass $M_{ZAMS}\lesssim 140\,M_\odot$ or $M_{ZAMS}\gtrsim 260\,M_\odot$; otherwise, they undergo fully disruption and end their life as Pair Instability Super Novae, leaving no remnant behind \parencite{Farmer_2019}. In recent years, the properties of this population have been significantly constrained through the detection of gravitational waves from merging compact binaries \parencite{Abbott_2016}, as well as by advances in microlensing techniques capable of identifying isolated BH candidates \parencite{Bennett_2002}.
On the opposite side $(\sim 10^6-10^9\,M_\odot)$, instead, Pulsar timing arrays has already probed the upper mass range of SMBHs population \parencite{Taylor_2021}, whose most accepted formation model interpret them as the final stage of a secular accretion onto initially lighter black hole seeds (see, e.g., \textcite{Peterson_book}).\\While substantial progress has been made in detecting and characterizing both stellar-mass BHs and SMBHs, the observational confirmation and detailed investigation of intermediate-mass black holes (IMBHs), lying in the mass range $\sim 10^2$--$10^5,M_\odot$, remain significantly more challenging. Although an increasing number of candidates have been identified in the local Universe, particularly in dwarf galaxies, through accretion signatures (see, e.g., \textcite{Reines_2013}), their sphere of influence—conventionally defined as the sphere enclosing a stellar mass equal to twice that of the central BH \parencite{Sesana_2015}, within which the BH gravitational potential dominates—is typically too small for reliable stellar or gas dynamical measurements, especially at higher redshift. Nevertheless, the discovery of high-redshift quasars (see, e.g., \textcite{Banados_2018}) points to the existence of BH seeds with masses comparable to those of IMBHs already in the early Universe, strengthening the idea that even the nuclei of less massive galaxies harbor MBHs. Therefore, it is common practice to infer the central BH mass by extrapolating the empirical correlation between BH mass and the stellar mass of the host galaxy's bulge, even for quiescent galactic nuclei (see, e.g., \textcite{Schutte_2019}; \textcite{Maiolino_2024}).\\Figure \ref{IMBH_Pathways} illustrates three distinct theoretical pathways for IMBH formation, highlighting the number of BHs expected to form through each channel, their corresponding mass ranges, and the redshift range over which each formation mechanism is expected to be most efficient. Each formation channel proceeds as follows \parencite{Greene_2020}:
\paragraph{Pop III.}
Sufficiently massive primordial stars, commonly referred to as Population III (Pop III) stars, may possess enough gravitational binding energy to withstand the energy released during the photodisintegration-induced collapse of their cores, ultimately leading to the formation of a BH remnant \parencite{Fryer_2001}.
\paragraph{Directe collapse.}
At high redshift, metal-free gas clouds may undergo nearly monolithic gravitational collapse without significant fragmentation, in contrast to what is typically observed in the present-day Universe, owing to the inefficient cooling of neutral hydrogen, and lead to the formation of IMBHs \parencite{Lodato_2006}.
\paragraph{Gravitational runaway.}
Runaway stellar collisions in high-density clusters may trigger a sequence of stellar mergers, progressively producing more massive stars that can undergo further collisions. The final product of this repeated mergers process may eventually collapse into an IMBH (see, e.g., \textcite{Rodriguez_2019}; \textcite{Devecchi_2009}).
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{IMBH_Review_Formation.png}
    \caption{Schematic representation of different BH seeding channels, from their formation and subsequent growth through mergers and accretion to the present day: Pop III stars (blue), direct collapse (red), and gravitational runaway (green). The corresponding present-day BH mass functions, occupation fractions, and BH–galaxy scaling relations are shown on the right. Figure taken from \textcite{Greene_2020}.}
    \label{IMBH_Pathways}
\end{figure}
Within the framework of the hierarchical growth scenario of galaxies \parencite{White_1978}, the formation of IMBH binaries is expected to occur naturally as a consequence of mergers between their host stellar systems \parencite{Maiolino_2024}. These systems are among the most powerful sources of gravitational waves (GWs) in the Universe \parencite{Maggiore_book}, making them prime targets for current and future GW observatories. In particular, the upcoming Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA), a joint ESA/NASA space mission, will probe the low-frequency GW regime, from $\sim 10^{-4}$ to $\sim 10^{-1}$ Hz \parencite{Amaro-Seoane_2017,Amaro-Seaone_2023}, providing access to compact-object binaries with masses of $\sim10^4-10^7,M_\odot$ and enabling the detection of their mergers out to redshift $z\sim20$ \parencite{Colpi_2024}. At higher frequencies, the ground-based Einstein Telescope (ET) will complement LISA by extending the accessible frequency and mass ranges towards lower-mass systems, being expected to detect IMBH binaries with masses of $\sim10^2-10^3,M_\odot$ out to redshifts of a few \parencite{ET_book}. Therefore, current and future GW observatories may provide a unique opportunity to unveil the population of IMBHs through their gravitational-wave emission, which may be detectable even when their electromagnetic signatures are too faint to be observed.\\In light of this perspective, this thesis investigates the time evolution of the main orbital parameters of IMBH binaries, with particular focus on the semimajor axis $a$ and eccentricity $e$, and examines how BH masses, stellar background properties, and the binary initial condition influence their dynamical evolution and coalescence timescale through a series of numerical experiments. To this end, the evolution of IMBH binaries is studied using two complementary approaches: the numerical integration of the differential equations for the binary separation and eccentricity provided by the hybrid model developed by \textcite{Quinlan_1996} and extended by \textcite{Sesana_2010}, and high-resolution, gas-free N-body simulations of mergers between BHs and their stellar hosts performed with the hybrid tree-regularized code KETJU \parencite{Mannerkoski_2023}.\\The suite of numerical experiments presented in \textcite{Khan_2021} and \textcite{Khan_2024} on both nucleated and non-nucleated dwarf galaxies have highlighted the crucial role of the stellar background density,particularly in the vicinity of the BHB sphere of influence, in driving fast and efficient mergers. In particular, high-density stellar environments ensure an adequate supply of low-angular-momentum stars, thereby sustaining efficient hardening via stellar slinghot (see, e.g., \textcite{Mikkola_1992}), while in low-density environments, the insufficient replenishment of such orbits may lead to binary stalling, giving rise to the so-called "last-parsec problem" \parencite{Milosavljevic_2003}. The evidence that different stellar environments produce peculiar pathways to coalescence highlights the need to investigate the dynamical evolution of IMBH binaries across a representative range of realistic stellar hosts. The first class considered here comprises dwarf galaxies, whose most commonly adopted definition relies on an upper limit of $M_V>-18$ in absolute (V)-band magnitude \textcite{Grebel_2003}. The observation of these systems even at high redshifts (see, e.g., \textcite{Nguyen_2018}; \parencite{Kikuchihara_2020}) makes them plausible stellar hosts at the time of IMBH pairing, whose GW emission may be probed by present-day observatories. Although N-body simulations have shown that IMBH binaries embedded in non-nucleated dwarf galaxies have coalescence times exceeding the Hubble time, and despite the fact that most dwarf galaxies harbor a nuclear star cluster (see, e.g., \textcite{Neumayer_2020}), we artificially evolve non-nucleated dwarf galaxies back to redshift $z$ according to the cosmic size evolution law derived by \textcite{Ono_2023}. Together with the observed increase in the BH-to-bulge mass ratio towards higher redshifts as shown in Figure \ref{BH-Bulge mass}, this effect is expected to partially compensate for the absence of a nuclear star cluster, potentially allowing the binary to coalesce within the present-day Universe.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Maiolino.png}
    \caption{Black hole mass as a function of host galaxy stellar mass. Golden circles denote the JADES results, blue symbols high-z JWST measurements, grey triangles high-z QSOs, and red symbols local galaxies. Dashed orange lines connect candidate merging BHs. Figure taken from \textcite{Maiolino_2024}.}
    \label{BH-Bulge mass}
\end{figure}
In recent years, several claims have been made regarding the possible presence of IMBHs in Galactic and extragalactic globular clusters (GCs). Although these detections are not always conclusive, dynamical measurements and signatures of accretion in the X-ray band have provided indications of central IMBHs in a number of old stellar clusters (e.g., \textcite{Gebhardt_2005}; \textcite{Baumgardt_2017}). At the same time, observations of galaxies at very high redshift are revealing stellar systems with properties that may provide insights into the possible progenitors of present-day GCs. An example of this is constituted by the "Misty Moons" \parencite{Eros_2026} and the "Bullet arc" \parencite{Bradac_2025}, two strongly lensed galaxies at a photometric redshift of \(z_{phot}\sim11-12\) and spectroscopic redshift $z_{spec}\sim11$ respectively, which are resolved into several massive stellar clumps. This suggests that star formation in the early universe may have been clump-dominated, with such stellar clumps potentially representing the progenitors of the more compact and less massive GCs observed in the local Universe, before undergoing substantial mass loss due to tidal interactions with their host galaxies. Figure \ref{size-mass} shows the size–mass relation of local star clusters, star-forming clumps, and dwarf galaxies, providing a direct comparison between the properties of local GCs and high-\(z\) stellar clumps.

    \label{size-mass}
\end{figure}
Mindful of these considerations, we also include stellar-clump-like backgrounds in our suite of numerical experiments, adopting IMBH masses consistent with the empirical scaling relation shown in Figure~\ref{BH-Bulge mass}.\\The structure of this thesis is outlined as follows.\\In \autoref{chap:chapter1}, we provide a brief overview of the main physical mechanisms promoting the shrinking of BH binaries, including violent relaxation, dynamical friction, stellar scattering by low-angular-momentum stars, and GW emission.\\In \autoref{chap:chapter2}, it will be introduced the self-consistent hybrid model developed by \textcite{Quinlan_1996} and \textcite{Sesana_2010}. We then present the evolutionary tracks obtained for the adopted sample of host galaxies and explore the relevant parameter space, highlighting how different environmental properties and initial conditions affect the binary evolution.\\In \autoref{chap:chapter3} are reported the main features of the codes used to generate equilibrium N-body initial conditions and carry out the numerical simulations. The chapter then presents the results obtained by combining the KETJU evolution, which follows the BH pair from early merger to the stellar-scattering-dominated regime, with the hybrid model used to track its subsequent evolution towards final coalescence.\\Finally, the main results of this work are summarized, and possible future improvements are reported in the concluding chapter.

\chapter{Physical Mechanisms of Binary Hardening}\label{chap:chapter1}




\vspace*{\fill}

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{0.65\textwidth}
\centering
\textit{In the "bottom-up" scenario, in which low-mass structures form first and progressively more massive systems assemble through hierarchical clustering and mergers, massive black hole binaries are expected to form as a consequence of mergers involving stellar systems or galaxies embedding such compact objects.\\This chapter reviews the main physical mechanisms driving the hardening of MBH binaries, from the initial merger of their hosts to the final coalescence. We first discuss violent relaxation, which efficiently converts the orbital energy of the merging systems into disordered kinetic energy of the newly formed system. Then, dynamical friction is considered. It acts on massive components such as MBHs and drags them toward the centre of the merger remnant, eventually leading to the formation of a gravitationally bound binary. The subsequent evolution is governed by three-body interactions with low-angular momentum stars: we discuss the gravitational slingshot mechanism, through which stellar ejections extract energy and angular momentum from the binary and promote its hardening, as well as the role of loss-cone refilling. Finally, we derive the key equations describing the binary orbital evolution once gravitational-wave emission becomes the dominant mechanism governing the orbital decay.}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

\section{Galaxy Merging and Violent Relaxation}
\label{sec:Galaxy Mergers and Violent Relaxation}
When two galaxies approach each other with sufficiently low relative velocity and small impact parameter, the encounter may result in a galaxy merger (see, e.g., \citealt{Toomre_1972};
\citealt{Cimatti_book}). Galaxy mergers can be broadly classified according to the physical properties of the systems involved. Of particular relevance here are dissipationless, or dry, mergers, which involve gas-poor galaxies and are characterized by negligible radiative energy losses, so that the total energy of the system is approximately conserved \parencite{Khochfar_2009}. Numerical $N$-body simulations have shown that collisions between galaxies of comparable mass, commonly referred to as "major mergers", generally result in a single, virialized remnant (see, e.g.  \citealt{Barnes_1992}; \citealt{Hilz_2012}). The formation of this remnant proceeds through a series of relaxation processes driven by the rapidly varying gravitational potential during the encounter, which redistributes the energy and angular momentum of the system and drives the initially out-of-equilibrium configuration toward a new equilibrium state (see, e.g. \textcite{Lynden_1967}; \textcite{Saslaw_1970}).\\In the scientific literature, several approaches have been proposed to define and derive the timescale of violent relaxation. Here, we follow the approach of \textcite{Lynden_1967} and define it as the characteristic oscillation period of the newly formed galaxy around the equilibrium configuration established by the virial condition
\begin{equation}\label{virial condition}
 2K+W=0,
\end{equation}
holding in its barycentric inertial reference system $S_{CM}$, with $K$ and $W$ standing for the total kinetic and gravitational potential energy of the galaxy remnant respectively. We therefore first characterize the equilibrium state attained by the system following the relaxation process.\\Let us consider two galaxies, each with mass $M_i$ and total energy $E_i=K_i+W_i$. In an ideal dissipationless merger, the total mass and energy are conserved, so that the resulting remnant has mass $M=M_1+M_2$ and energy $E=E_1+E_2+E_{\rm orb}$, where $E_{orb}$ accounts for the orbital energy of the pair, including both its ordered kinetic and mutual gravitational contributions. Following violent relaxation on a timescale $T_r$, the remnant settles into an equilibrium configuration that can be characterized by the virial velocity dispersion $\sigma_v$ and gravitational radius $r_g$, defined by
\begin{equation}
\sigma_v^2=\frac{2K}{M}, \qquad r_g=-\frac{GM^2}{W},
\end{equation}
which provide proxies for the characteristic stellar velocity dispersion and size of the galaxy in equilibrium, thus representing key information on the final stage reached by the merging galaxies.\\By combining the virial condition (\ref{virial condition})—which applies both to the progenitor galaxies prior to the interaction and to the remnant after the violent relaxation timescale $T_r$—with energy conservation, one obtains
\begin{equation}\label{remnant_virial_sigma}
\sigma_v^2=\frac{M_1\sigma_{v,1}^2+M_2\sigma_{v,2}^2-2E_{\rm orb}}{M_1+M_2}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{remnant_gravitational_radius}
\frac{(M_1+M_2)^2}{r_g}=\frac{M_1^2}{r_{g,1}}+\frac{M_2^2}{r_{g,2}}-\frac{2E_{\rm orb}}{G},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{v,i}$ and $r_{g,i}$ denote the virial velocity dispersion and the gravitational radius, respectively, of the colliding systems \parencite{Cimatti_book}. From Equations~(\ref{remnant_virial_sigma}) and~(\ref{remnant_gravitational_radius}), it follows that parabolic encounters between identical progenitors, defined by $E_{orb}=0$, which are the configuration considered in this thesis to carry out $N$-body numerical experiments as shown in chapter \ref{chap:chapter3}, give rise to a galaxy remnant having
\begin{equation}
M=2M_1=2M_2, \qquad r_g=2r_{g,1}=2r_{g,2}, \qquad \sigma_v=\sigma_{v,1}=\sigma_{v,2}.
\end{equation}
Therefore, before the merger to occur, each progenitor galaxy, which is assumed to be made of $N$ stars of masses $m_i$ and velocities $\mathbf{v_i}$, is individually virialized and satisfies the virial equilibrium condition
\begin{equation}
2\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{1}{2} m_i v_i^2-\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j \ne i} \frac{Gm_i m_j}{|\mathbf{x_i}-\mathbf{x_j}|}=2K+W=0.
\label{extended virial condition}
\end{equation}
Neverthless, this condition is temporarily violated once the collision begins, as the newly formed remnant is driven far from equilibrium by the coherent motion inherited from the relative orbital motion of the progenitors. Consequently, during violent relaxation, Equation (\ref{virial condition}) is no longer satisfied at every instant; rather, the system obeys the time-dependent virial theorem,
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{2}\ddot{I}=2K+W,
\label{time_dependent_virial}
\end{equation}
where $I$ denotes the polar moment of inertia.\\To quantitatively characterize the level of virialization of any system evolving towards equilibrium, it is usefull to introduce the so-called virial ratio
\begin{equation}
Q\equiv \frac{2K}{|W|}.
\end{equation}
Figure \ref{Virial ratio image} shows the results of $N$-body simulations of dissipationless galaxy mergers (see \citealt{Nipoti_Londrillo_2007}), focusing on the evolution of the virial ratio $Q$. The oscillations of $Q$ have been reconstructed both within the framework of Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) and under Newtonian gravity. In the former case, the standard Poisson equation is replaced by the non-relativistic field equation \parencite{Bekenstein_1984}
\begin{equation}
\nabla\cdot\left[\mu\left(\frac{\|\nabla\phi\|}{a_0}\right)\nabla\phi\right]=4\pi G\rho,
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ denotes the MOND gravitational potential, $\rho$ is the mass density, $a_0\simeq1.2\times10^{-10}\,m/s^2$ is the characteristic acceleration scale of MOND, and $\mu(y)=y/\sqrt{1+y^2}$  \parencite{Milgrom_1983}.\\Although the merging timescales are significantly longer in MOND than in Newtonian gravity with dark matter, in the Newtonian case the amplitude of the virial ratio oscillations rapidly decreases with time as $Q$ approaches its equilibrium value, $Q=1$, owing to the progressive conversion of ordered kinetic energy into random stellar motions. As illustrated by the green curve, the system approaches virial equilibrium through a series of progressively damped oscillations, with most of the virialization process occurring in the vicinity of the equilibrium value. We will take advantage of this observed behaviour for estimating the timescale $T_r$ over which violent relaxation takes place.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{Nipoti_Londrillo_2007.png}
    \caption{Time evolution of the virial ratio (top panels) and of
the barycentric relative speed (bottom panels) for the two sets of galaxy merger simulations: head-on (left panels) and off-centre (right panels). Blue, red, and green curves refer to MOND, equivalent Newtonian, and purely baryonic Newtonian simulations, respectively. Figure taken from \textcite{Nipoti_Londrillo_2007}.}
    \label{Virial ratio image}
\end{figure}
To this end, it is useful to start from the physical mechanism underlying violent relaxation and consider the evolution of the energy $\epsilon_*$ of an individual star moving in a time-dependent gravitational potential $\psi(\mathbf{x},t)$. We first show that the stellar energy can change only as a consequence of an explicit time dependence of the gravitational potential. Thus, if the potential is chosen to vanish at infinity, the energy $\epsilon_*$ of a star with mass $m_*$ and velocity $\mathbf{v}$ is given by
\begin{equation}\label{Stellar energy}
 \epsilon_*=\frac{1}{2}m_*v^2+m_*\psi(\mathbf{x},t).
\end{equation}
Differentiating both sides of Equation~(\ref{Stellar energy}) with respect to time yields
\begin{equation}
 \frac{d\epsilon_*}{dt}=m_*\left[\mathbf{v}\cdot \dot{\mathbf{v}}+\nabla\psi \cdot \mathbf{v} +\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}\right].
\end{equation}
For a star moving under the gravitational potential $\psi$, its acceleration satisfies $\dot{\mathbf{v}}=-\nabla\psi$ and the previous equation reduces to
\begin{equation}\label{stellar energy derivative}
\frac{d\epsilon}{dt}=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t},
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon=\epsilon_*/m_*$ is the stellar energy per unit mass. Equation~(\ref{stellar energy derivative}) shows that individual stars continuously gain and lose orbital energy as a consequence of the rapidly varying gravitational field. This naturally motivates defining the violent relaxation timescale $T_r$ as the timescale over which the energy change of a typical star becomes comparable to its initial energy \parencite{Lynden_1967}, i.e.
\begin{equation}
T_r =
\left\langle
\frac{\left( d\epsilon / dt \right)^2}{\epsilon^2}
\right\rangle^{-1/2}
=
\left\langle
\frac{\left( \partial \psi / \partial t \right)^2}{\epsilon^2}
\right\rangle^{-1/2}.
\label{violent_relaxation_time}
\end{equation}
As discussed above, during violent relaxation, the remnant progressively approaches virial equilibrium set by $Q=1$, as indicated by the damped oscillations of the virial ratio around this value. However, in this regime $\ddot{I}$ remains small but non-zero and a mean star experiences changes in both kinetic and gravitational energies in a coupled manner, approximately satisfying
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{2}m_*v^2 \sim - \frac{1}{4}m_*\psi,
\label{mean_star_kinetic_potential_relation}
\end{equation}
as dictated by Equation (\ref{extended virial condition}).\\By substituting Equation~(\ref{mean_star_kinetic_potential_relation}) into Equation~(\ref{Stellar energy}), we can express the specific stellar energy solely in terms of the gravitational potential and, mindful of Equation (\ref{violent_relaxation_time}), obtain the following equivalent form for the violent relaxation timescale:
\begin{equation}
T_r \equiv \frac{3}{4}
\left\langle
\frac{\psi^2}{\dot{\psi}^{2}}
\right\rangle^{1/2}.
\label{violent_relaxation_time_reformulation}
\end{equation}
Dimensional analysis suggests that the polar moment of inertia can be expressed in terms of the total mass $M$ and the square of the instantaneous characteristic radius $R(t)$ as
\begin{equation}
I(t)=\lambda^2 MR^2(t),
\label{moment_of_inertia}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda^2$ is a dimensionless structural factor of order unity that encodes the instantaneous mass distribution and is determined by the geometrical configuration of the masses $m_i$.\\Let us consider small radial perturbations of the system about its virial equilibrium configuration. In this regime, the instantaneous characteristic radius of the system can be expressed as a small deviation from its equilibrium value, which we identify with the gravitational radius. Therefore, to the first order,
\begin{equation}
R(t)\approx r_g+\delta R,
\end{equation}
with $|\delta R|\ll r_g$ representing the small radial displacement from equilibrium. In this regime, linearizing Equation (\ref{virial condition}) with respect to the perturbation gives
\begin{equation}
2\lambda^2r_g\ddot{\delta R}
-\frac{GM}{r_g^2}\delta R,
\end{equation}
which we recognize to be that of a simple harmonic oscillator, with angular frequency
\begin{equation}
\omega=\left(
\frac{GM}{2\lambda^2r_g^3}
\right)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
It is worth of noticing that, introducing the galaxy mean density $\bar{\rho}=3M/(4\pi r_g^3)$, the latter quantity can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
\omega=
\left(
2\pi G\bar{\rho}
\right)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
Since the gravitational potential scales as $\psi\sim 1/r$, its time variation is governed by the same characteristic timescale as the radial oscillations, i.e. $\sim \omega^{-1}$. Plugging this scaling into the definition of the violent relaxation timescale in Equation (\ref{violent_relaxation_time_reformulation}) returns
\begin{equation}
T_r\simeq
\frac{3}{4\omega}=\frac{3}{\sqrt{32\pi G\bar{\rho}}}.
\label{violent_relaxation_time_final}
\end{equation}
Thus, the violent relaxation timescale is directly related to the characteristic dynamical timescale of the newly formed system and is expected to be of the order of its free-fall timescale $t_{ff}\propto 1/\sqrt{G\bar{\rho}}$.\\To obtain an order-of-magnitude estimate of this timescale for the merger of two identical non-nucleated dwarf galaxies, we may consider the non-nucleated galaxy model built by \textcite{Khan_2024} from a sample of non-nucleated dwarf galaxies ofthe Fornax cluster. For a galaxy of stellar mass $M_*=10^8\times M_\odot$, effective radius $R_e=0.56\,kpc$ and Sersic index $n=0.81$ (see Section \ref{sec:NGC 205 IMBH Binary evolution}), the violent relaxation timescale is expected to be of the order of $T_r=21.2\,Myr$. This estimate has been obtained from Equation (\ref{violent_relaxation_time_final}) by considering the average stellar density of the mentioned reference galaxy within its effective radius. This result demonstrates that this mechanism can redistribute the orbital energy of the progenitor galaxies on a much shorter timescale than that associated with two-body relaxation $t_{2b}$. The latter will be introduced in \S~\ref{sec:Two-Body Relaxation and Dynamical Friction} as a key process responsible for replenishing the so-called binary "loss cone".










\section{Two-Body Relaxation and Dynamical Friction}
\label{sec:Two-Body Relaxation and Dynamical Friction}
Galaxies are multi-component systems in dynamical equilibrium, where different stellar populations, gaseous phases, dust, and dark matter coexist \parencite{Cimatti_book}. These environments host an enormous number of stars, which makes it impossible to track the orbit of each element. Neverthless, by abandoning the idea of characterizing the time evolution of each degree of freedom in the phase-space (PhS), a probabilistic description of the stellar population is still possible in terms of the one-particle distribution function (DF) $f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)$. This can be interpreted as the probability density of finding a star at a given point $\mathbf{w}=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})$ in PhS at time $t$. It is conventionally normalized so that
\begin{equation}
\rho(\mathbf{x},t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^3}d^3\mathbf{v}\,f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t),
\end{equation}
where $\rho(\mathbf{x},t)$ is the stellar density providing a smooth approximation to the actual, discrete distribution of $N$ stars.\\In the collisionless approximation, DF is conserved along the orbits of the gravitational potential $\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)$ associated with the stellar density $\rho(\mathbf{x},t)$. In this regime, it thus obeys the so-called Collisionless Boltzmann Equation (CBE),
\begin{equation}\label{CBE}
\frac{df}{dt}\equiv \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}\cdot \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{x}}+\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}\cdot \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{v}}=0.
\end{equation}
Equation (\ref{CBE}) is expected to hold as long as the effects of repeated gravitational encounters between stars remain negligible over the age of the system. Although individual encounters are typically weak, their cumulative effect can progressively redistribute energy and angular momentum among stars, causing their trajectories to deviate from those drawn by the smooth potential $\Phi$ and DF to diffuse in PS \parencite{Binney_book}.\\The characteristic timescale over which these cumulative encounters significantly alter stellar trajectories is the two-body relaxation time $t_{2b}$, which provides a natural criterion for assessing the validity of the collisionless approximation. Systems whose age is shorter than their two-body relaxation time, i.e $t_{age}<t_{2b}$, are conventionally referred to as collisionless; otherwise, they will be referred to as collisional \parencite{Ciotti_book}.\\The approach of \textcite{Chandrasekhar_1943} for estimating $t_{2b}$ relies on the impulsive approximation. It consists of two main assumptions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Stellar encounters are treated as independent events, so that correlations between consecutive encounters and simultaneous multiple interactions are neglected.
\item The gravitational influence of the remaining $N-2$ stars is neglected during each encounter, reducing the problem to a sequence of isolated hyperbolic two-body interactions.
\end{enumerate}
Let us work under the simplifying assumption of a spatially homogeneous distribution of field stars with isotropic velocity field. The DF $f(\mathbf{x_f},\mathbf{v_f})$ of such system can be expressed as the product of the position-independent number density $n_f$ and probability density function $g(v_f)$ in the velocity space,
\begin{equation}
    f(\mathbf{x_f},\mathbf{v_f})=n_fg(v_f).
\end{equation}
Two-body encounters between a test particle of mass $m_t$ and a field star of mass $m_f$ induce small changes in an orbital property $P$ of the test particle. Although each interaction produces only a small perturbation, the cumulative effect of many encounters causes $P$ to diffuse. This process is quantified by the diffusion coefficient $D(P)$, which measures the rate at which $P$ changes per unit time. Owing to the Impulsive Approximation, it can be written as
\begin{equation}\label{diffusion coeff def}
    D(P)\equiv 2\pi n_f\int_{\mathbb{R}^3} d^3\mathbf{v_f}\|\mathbf{v_t}-\mathbf{v_f}\|g(v_f)\int_0^\infty db\langle P\rangle b,
 \end{equation}
where $\langle P\rangle$ denotes the angle-averaged value of the orbital property $P$, and $b$ is the impact parameter of the hyperbolic encounter \parencite{Ciotti_book}.
Since significant deflections of the original orbits are expected beyond $t_{2b}$, corresponding to the onset of the collisional regime, a useful indicator for determining $t_{2b}$ may be the orbital property $P=\Delta\mathbf{v}_{t\perp}$, that is the component of the test-particle velocity change perpendicular to the initial relative velocity with respect to the field particle. However, under the assumption of isotropy, the first-order diffusion coefficients of the velocity components vanish \parencite{Binney_book}. Consequently, the leading non-vanishing contribution is represented by the second-order diffusion coefficient, hence, making the quantity $P=\|\Delta \mathbf{v}_{t\perp}\|^2$ the relevant orbital parameter for our analysis. By taking advantage of the small-angle scattering approximation (see, e.g., \textcite{Chandrasekhar_1943}),
\begin{equation}\label{small angle approximation}
    \|\Delta \mathbf{v}_{t\perp} \|\simeq \frac{2Gm_f}{vb},
\end{equation}
with $v$ denoting the modulus of the relative velocity between $m_t$ and $m_f$ at infinity,
and Equation (\ref{diffusion coeff def}) becomes
\begin{equation}\label{two divergences}
    D(\|\Delta \mathbf{v}_{t\perp} \|^2)\simeq 8\pi G^2m_f^2n_f\int_{\mathbb{R}^3}d^3\mathbf{v_f}\frac{g(v_f)}{v}\int_0^\infty \frac{db}{b}.
\end{equation}
The integral in Equation (\ref{two divergences}) exhibits two logarithmic divergences. The former, known as ultraviolet (UV) divergence, arises in the limit $b\rightarrow 0$ and results from extending the small-angle scattering approximation to encounters with sufficiently small impact parameters, where it breaks down. The latter, the infrared (IR) divergence, arises in the limit $b\rightarrow\infty$ and is a consequence of modelling the stellar system as infinite and homogeneous. The UV divergence is avoided by replacing the small-angle approximated solution in Equation (\ref{small angle approximation}) with the exact two-body scattering solution; mathematically, this operation is equivalento to impose a lower cutoff to the impact parameter equals $b_{\pi/2}(v)$, corresponding to the impact parameter associated with a deflection angle of $\pi/2$. The IR divergence cannot be eliminated and thus we have to set a fiducial  upper cutoff $b_{\max}$.\\The resulting diffusion coefficient can then be written as
\begin{equation}\label{diffusion with C log}
D(\|\Delta \mathbf{v}_{t\perp}\|^2) \sim 8\pi G^2 n_f m_f^2 \int_{\mathbb{R}^3} \frac{g(v_f)\ln \Lambda}{v} \, d^3 \mathbf{v}_f,
\end{equation}
where $\ln{(\Lambda)}=\ln{(b_{max}/b_{\pi/2})}$, is the so-called Coulomb logarithm. Although it depends on test-field particle relative velocity through $b_{\pi/2}(v)$, corresponding to a deflection angle of $\pi/2$, by defining the velocity-weighted Coulomb logarithm $\ln{(\bar{\Lambda})}$ so that
\begin{equation}
\int_{\mathbb{R}^3} \frac{g(v_f)\ln \Lambda}{\|\mathbf{v}_t - \mathbf{v}_f\|} \, d^3 \mathbf{v}_f
\equiv \Psi(v_t)\ln{\bar{\Lambda}},
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}\label{Rosenbluth potential}
    \Psi(v_t)=\int_{\mathcal{R}^3}\frac{g(v_f)}{\|\mathbf{v_t}-\mathbf{v_f}\|}d^3\mathbf{v_f},
\end{equation}
being the Rosenbluth potential, Equation (\ref{diffusion with C log}) turns into
\begin{equation}
D(\|\Delta \mathbf{v}_{t\perp}\|^2) \sim 8\pi G^2 n_f m_f^2 \Psi(v_t)\ln{\bar{\Lambda}}.
\end{equation}
One of the major simplifications introduced by the assumption of isotropy is that $\Psi(v_t)$ can be expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{Rosenbluth potential if isotropy}
\Psi(v_t) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^3} \frac{g(v_f)}{\|\mathbf{v}_t - \mathbf{v}_f\|} \, d^3 \mathbf{v}_f
\equiv \frac{\Xi(v_t)}{v_t} + 4\pi \int_{v_t}^{\infty} g(v_f) v_f \, dv_f,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\Xi(v_t) = 4\pi \int_{0}^{v_t} g(v_f) v_f^2 \, dv_f
\end{equation}
is normalized to unity as a consequence of the normalization of velocity-space distribution function $g(v_f)$.\\Finally, if one defines the two-body relaxation time $t_{2b}$ as the ratio between the orbital parameter $P=\|\Delta \mathbf{v}_{t\perp}\|^2$ and the associated diffusion coefficient, it turns out that
\begin{equation}\label{t_2b}
    t_{2b}\equiv\frac{v_t^2(-\infty)}{D(\Delta E_{t\perp})}\sim \frac{v_t^3}{8\pi G^2n_fm_f^2ln\bar\Lambda},
\end{equation}
holding in the high velocity limit from Equation (\ref{Rosenbluth potential if isotropy}).
Keeping in mind the estimate of $T_r$ obtained in \S~\ref{sec:Galaxy Mergers and Violent Relaxation} for a representative non-nucleated dwarf galaxy, we now compare it with the corresponding two-body relaxation time, $t_{2b}$, of the same system. An order-of-magnitude estimate can be obtained from
\begin{equation}\label{t_2b approx}
t_{2b}\approx t_{cross}\frac{N}{12\log{N}},
\end{equation}
where $N$ denotes the number of stars and $t_{cross}=2\sigma_v/R_e$ \parencite{Ciotti_book}. Assuming equal-mass stars with $m_*=1,M_\odot$, Equation (\ref{t_2b approx}) yields a $t_{2b}$ of the order of tens of Gyr, thus significantly exceeding the corresponding value of $T_r$.\\As discussed above, two-body encounters redistribute the test particle's kinetic energy, increasing its velocity component perpendicular to the initial direction of motion. In this sense, two-body interactions act as a heating mechanism in the transverse direction. Conversely, dynamical friction produces a systematic loss of kinetic energy along the direction of motion and behaving as cooling process(see, e.g., \citealt{Chandrasekhar_1943}; \citealt{Binney_book}). This results in the sinking of massive objects, such as an intruding secondary BH, and \(N\)-body simulations by \textcite{Baumgardt_2006} have demonstrated that dynamical friction efficiently drives the secondary BH down to $a_0$, defined as the radius enclosing a stellar mass equal to twice that of $M_2$, that is
\begin{equation}
\label{a_0 definition}
 M_*(<a_0)=2M_2.
\end{equation}
In order to derive an estimate for the dynamical friction timescale $t_{df}$, we follow the same approach and notation adopted above and first calculate the diffusion coefficient associated with the orbital property $P=\Delta \mathbf{v_t}$ for a test particle of mass $m_t$ moving through a stellar field composed of particles of mass $m_f$, i.e.
\begin{equation}\label{velocity first order diffusion coefficient}
    \frac{d\mathbf{v_t}}{dt}=D[\Delta \mathbf{v_t}]=-4\pi G^2 m_t m_f\ln{\Lambda}\int d^3\mathbf{v_f}f(\mathbf{v_f})\frac{\mathbf{v_t}-\mathbf{v_f}}{|\mathbf{v_t}-\mathbf{v_f}|^3}.
\end{equation}
Assuming an isotropic velocity field, Equation (\ref{velocity first order diffusion coefficient}) simplifies to
\begin{equation}\label{Chandrasekhar dynamical friction equation}
    \frac{d\mathbf{v_t}}{dt}=-16\pi^2 G^2 m_t m_f\ln{\Lambda}\left[\int_{0}^{v_t}dv_f\, v_f^2 f(v_f)\right]\frac{\mathbf{v_t}}{v_t^3},
\end{equation}
known as Chandrasekhar's dynamical friction formula \parencite{Chandrasekhar_1943}. Equation (\ref{Chandrasekhar dynamical friction equation}) shows that the effect of dynamical friction is to systematically decreases the test particle's velocity through an acceleration directed antiparallel to its velocity vector.\\By following an analogous derivation to that used to estimate the two-body relaxation timescale $t_{2b}$ in Equation (\ref{t_2b}), the dynamical friction timescale $t_{df}$ can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
 t_{df}\equiv \frac{\|\mathbf{v_t}(-\infty)\|}{\|D(\Delta \mathbf{v_{t\parallel}})\|}\sim \frac{v_t^3}{4\pi G^2 n_fm_f(m_f+m_t)\ln{(\bar{\Lambda})}\Xi(v_t)},
\end{equation}
We conclude this Section by deriving, under physically motivated assumptions, an analytic expression for the dynamical friction timescale $t_{df}$ of an intruding secondary BH of mass $m_2$ and velocity $\mathbf{v_2}$, which sinks towards the center of a stellar background hosting a primary BH of mass $m_1$. Let us assume the stellar distribution to possess a Maxwellian velocity distribution and a power-law density profile
\begin{equation}\label{Power low distribution}
    \rho(r)=\rho_0\left(\frac{r}{r_0}\right)^{-\gamma}.
\end{equation}
We begin by noting that for a Maxwellian velocity distribution, the integral over the stellar velocity distribution appearing in Equation (\ref{Chandrasekhar dynamical friction equation}) can be evaluated analytically, yielding
\begin{equation}\label{Chandrasekhar dynamical friction equation for MB}
    \frac{d\mathbf{v_2}}{dt}=-4\pi \ln{\Lambda}\left[erf(X)-\frac{2X}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-X^2}\right]\frac{G^2m_2\rho(r)}{v_2^3}\mathbf{v_2}
\end{equation}
where 
\begin{equation}\label{error function}
    erf(X)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{X}dt \, e^{-t^2}
\end{equation}
is the so-called error function. Here, $X\equiv v_c/(\sqrt{2}\sigma)$ is a dimensionless parameter that combines the circular velocity $v_c$ of the test particle with the velocity dispersion $\sigma$ of the stellar background.\\For an intruder BH moving along quasi-circular orbits within the sphere of influence of the primary BH, the angular momentum and the dynamical friction force acting on it are given by
\begin{equation}\label{dynamical friction angular momentum}
    \tilde{L}=m_2\sqrt{Gm_1r}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{dynamical friction force}
  \tilde{F}=m_2\frac{dv_2}{dt}=-C(\Lambda,X)\frac{G^2m_2^2\rho(r)}{v_c^2}
\end{equation}
respectively. Here, $C(\Lambda,X)$ is the dimensionless factor implicitly defined through the Equations (\ref{Chandrasekhar dynamical friction equation for MB}) and (\ref{dynamical friction force}).\\At this point, we can exploit the fact that, within the primary BH's sphere of influence, the circular velocity can be approximated as
\begin{equation}\label{dynamical friction v_c}
 v_c^2=\frac{Gm_1}{r}.
\end{equation}
Under these assumptions, the second cardinal equation of dynamics takes the form
\begin{equation}\label{dynamical friction angular momentum loss eq}
    \frac{d\tilde{L}}{dt}=\tilde{F}r,
\end{equation}
and leads to the differential equation for \(r=r(t)\) upon substituting the expressions given in Equations (\ref{dynamical friction force}), (\ref{dynamical friction angular momentum}) and (\ref{dynamical friction v_c}) (see, e.g., \citealt{Baumgardt_2006}; \citealt{Gualandris_2022}):
\begin{equation}\label{r(t) diff. eq. for dynamical friction}
    r^{-5/2}\frac{dr}{dt}=-2C(\Lambda,X(r))\frac{\sqrt{G}m_t}{m_f^{3/2}}\rho(r).
\end{equation}
Equation (\ref{r(t) diff. eq. for dynamical friction}) admits an analytic solution for an isotropic power-law density profile. Indeed, in this case
\begin{equation}
 X=\sqrt{\gamma-1}
\end{equation}
and is therefore independent of radius \parencite{Madigan_2011}. Integrating the differential equation subject to the initial condition $r(t=0)=r_0<r_{inf}$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{r(t) of stellar cusp under dynamical friction}
r(t)=
\begin{cases}
r_0\left[1-2C(\Lambda,X)\frac{\sqrt{G}m_2}{m_1^{3/2}}\left(\gamma-\frac{3}{2}\right)\rho_0r_0^{3/2}t\right]^{\frac{1}{\gamma-3/2}}
& \text{if } 1<\gamma\le 3 \land \gamma \ne \frac{3}{2} \\[6pt]
r_0\exp\left[-2C(\Lambda,X)\frac{\sqrt{G}m_2}{m_1^{3/2}}\rho_0r_0^{3/2}t\right]
& \text{if } \gamma = \frac{3}{2}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Equation (\ref{r(t) of stellar cusp under dynamical friction}) applies to an isotropic, power-law stellar background, or more generally to a stellar distribution that approaches such a density profile within the sphere of influence of the central BH and provides an estimate of the duration of the final stage of dynamical friction, from $r_{inf}$ down to $a_0$. Indeed, setting the reference radius $r_0$ and density $\rho_0$ to their respective values at the influence radius, and defining $t_{\rm fric}$ such that $r(t_{\rm fric})=a_0$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{t_fric of stellar cusp}
t_{\rm fric}=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle
\frac{m_1^{3/2}}
{2C(\Lambda,X)\sqrt{G}\,m_2\,\rho_{\rm inf}r_{\rm inf}^{\gamma}}
\frac{r_{\rm inf}^{\gamma-3/2}-a_0^{\gamma-3/2}}
{\gamma-\frac{3}{2}},
& \text{if } \gamma\neq\frac{3}{2}, \\[10pt]
\displaystyle
\frac{m_1^{3/2}}
{2C(\Lambda,X)\sqrt{G}\,m_2\,\rho_{\rm inf}r_{\rm inf}^{3/2}}
\ln\left(\frac{r_{\rm inf}}{a_0}\right),
& \text{if } \gamma=\frac{3}{2}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
We now seek to determine both $r_{inf}$ and $a_0$ for a power-law stellar distribution hosting a BH binary of total mass $M_{12}=m_1+m_2$. In this case, the influence radius $r_{inf}$, defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{r_inf definition}
 M_*(<r_{inf})=2M_{12},
\end{equation}
and $a_0$, defined by Equation (\ref{a_0 definition}),
admit an analytical expression. They turn to be
\begin{equation}
 r_{inf}=r_0\left[\frac{(3-\gamma)M_{12}}{2\pi \rho_0r_0^3}\right]^{1/(3-\gamma)}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\label{a_0=a_0(r_inf)}
 a_0=r_{inf}\left(\frac{q}{1+q}\right)^{1/(3-\gamma)},
\end{equation}
respectively, with $q=m_2/m_1<1$ denoting the BH masses ratio. Equation (\ref{a_0=a_0(r_inf)}) shows that, at fixed power-law slope $\gamma$, $a_0$ spans the range from $0$ to $r_{inf}2^{1/\gamma-3}$ as $q$ varies from $0$ to $1$.\\From $a_0$ onward, dynamical friction, which has driven the orbital decay and pairing of the BHs up to this point, becomes inefficient and the further hardening of the binary becomes primarily driven by individual encounters with low-angular-momentum stars that are unbound to the binary and can extract energy and angular momentum from it. We address this shrinking mechanism in \S~\ref{sec:Gravitational Slingshot Effect}.









\section{Black Hole Binary–Star Three-Body Scattering}\label{sec:Gravitational Slingshot Effect}
As discussed in \S~\ref{sec:Galaxy Mergers and Violent Relaxation} and \S~\ref{sec:Two-Body Relaxation and Dynamical Friction}, during a dry, major merger, the remnant evolves towards equilibrium on the violent relaxation timescale $T_r$, while the MBHs initially located at the centers of the progenitor galaxies sink towards the center of the remnant through dynamical friction on a timescale $t_{fric}$. As their separation decreases, the MBHs eventually cross each other sphere of influence and form a gravitationally bound pair at a separation of order $a_0$, defined as the binary separation at which the enclosed stellar mass equals the secondary BH mass $M_2$ \parencite{Baumgardt_2006}.
At this stage, the binary separation is sufficiently small that distant stars mainly perturb the binary center of mass, with negligible effect on its orbital energy \parencite{Quinlan_1996}. Further hardening is therefore driven predominantly by low angular momentum stars passing in the vicinity of the binary and extracting both energy and angular momentum from it. Each time such a star---belonging to the so-called binary "loss cone", as we will discuss in more detail in \S~\ref{sec:Loss Cone}---passes sufficiently close to the binary, a three-body encounter takes place. Under suitable conditions, this interaction produces hyper-velocity stars (see, e.g., \citealt{Quinlan_1996}; \citealt{Sesana_2006}) through the so-called stellar slingshot mechanism \parencite{Mikkola_1992}. In this section, we review the main physical aspects of three-body scattering and the stellar slingshot mechanism, which are key processes driving the hardening of MBHBs.\\We begin by considering the so-called "two-body problem" and reviewing some of its key properties, which will be useful in understanding the physics of the stellar slingshot. Adopting the same notation as in the previous Section, let us consider a test particle and a field particle, with masses $m_t$ and $m_f$, respectively, interacting solely through gravity. Introducing the total mass of the system, $M=m_t+m_f$, and the reduced mass, $\mu=m_tm_f/M$, the total energy in the mass centre frame $S_{CM}$ is given by
\begin{equation}
\label{E_in_S_cm}
    E=\frac{1}{2}\mu\|\mathbf{v}\|^2-\frac{Gm_fm_t}{r},
\end{equation}
 where  $r(t)\equiv \|\mathbf{x_t}-\mathbf{x_f}\|$ and $\mathbf{v}(t)\equiv \mathbf{v_t}-\mathbf{v_f}$ denote particles relative separation and velocity.\\For two unbound particles moving along hyperbolic orbits in $S_{CM}$, energy conservation, as expressed by Equation (\ref{E_in_S_cm}), evaluated in the asymptotic limit $r(-\infty)=r(+\infty)\rightarrow+\infty$, yields
\begin{equation}\label{relative velocity modulus conservation}
     \|\mathbf{v}(-\infty)\|=  \|\mathbf{v}(+\infty)\|.
\end{equation}
Although it is not conserved along the entire orbit, Equation (\ref{relative velocity modulus conservation}) shows that the magnitude of the relative velocity---i.e., the velocity of the test particle as measured by an observer comoving with the field particle---is the same at the two asymptotic limits. Therefore, the encounter results only in a rotation of the velocity vector $\mathbf{v}$ and the corresponding velocity change $\Delta\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}(+\infty)-\mathbf{v}(-\infty)$ can be conveniently decomposed  as the sum of the components perpendicular and parallel to the initial relative velocity $\mathbf{v}(-\infty)$ as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{rel vel decomposition}
    \mathbf{v}(+\infty)=\mathbf{v}(-\infty)+\Delta\mathbf{v}_\parallel +\Delta\mathbf{v}_\perp.
\end{equation}
Substituting the expression for $\mathbf{v}(+\infty)$ given in Equation (\ref{rel vel decomposition}) into Equation (\ref{relative velocity modulus conservation}) leads to the identity
\begin{equation}\label{slingshot negative scalar product}
     \| \Delta\mathbf{v}_\parallel\|^2+  \| \Delta\mathbf{v}_\perp\|^2+\langle \Delta\mathbf{v}_\parallel, \mathbf{v}(-\infty) \rangle=0.
\end{equation}
A first inspection of Equation (\ref{slingshot negative scalar product}) reveals that a necessary condition for it to hold is that the scalar product be negative. This observation will be crucial for assessing whether a binary BH--star interaction results in binary hardening or softening \parencite{Ciotti_book}.\\The results derived above impose a constraint on the relative velocity of the interacting particles in a specific inertial frame. However, the interaction can be described in any inertial frame $S_0$. Indeed, while Equation (\ref{relative velocity modulus conservation}) shows that both the total energy and the individual mechanical energies of the particles are conserved between the two asymptotic limits, the close encounter generally induces changes in the individual energies $E_t$ and $E_f$ in $S_0$.\\Between the two configurations at infinite relative separation, the test particle undergoes a net variation in mechanical energy,
\begin{equation}\label{t energy variation 1}
\Delta E_t=\frac{1}{2}m_t[|\mathbf{v_t}(+\infty)|^2-  |\mathbf{v_t}(-\infty)|^2]=-\Delta E_f,
\end{equation}
which is exactly compensated by the corresponding variation in the mechanical energy of the field particle, such that
\begin{equation}
\Delta E= \Delta E_t+\Delta E_f=0.
\end{equation}
By combinig the velocity composition law
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{v_t}(t)=\mathbf{v_{CM}}+\frac{\mu}{m_t}\mathbf{v}(t)
\end{equation}
with the conservation of the center-of-mass velocity vector $\mathbf{v_{CM}}$, which holds for isolated systems, Equation (\ref{t energy variation 1}) can be written as \parencite{Ciotti_book}
\begin{equation}
    \Delta E_t=\mu\langle \Delta\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v_{CM}} \rangle.
\end{equation}
We are now in a position to address three-body scattering involving a gravitationally bound BH binary and an incoming star. The dynamics of this encounter can be understood by considering the limiting case of a strongly asymmetric binary in which $M_1\gg M_2\gg m_*$ so that both the star and the secondary BH, having masses $m_*$ and $M_2$, can travel along nearly Keplerian orbits around $M_1$ with slightly different radii and, consequently, possess slightly different Keplerian velocities. The secondary BH acts as the field particle, while the star plays the role of the test particle. The primary BH serves only to establish the relative velocity between the two bodies. As a result, the center of mass motion of this gravitationally unbound pair is essentially determined by the orbit of $M_2$ around $M_1$. Therefore, the approximation
\begin{equation}\label{Primary BH approx CM}
    \mathbf{x_{CM}}\simeq \mathbf{x_f}, \qquad\mathbf{v_{CM}}\simeq \mathbf{v_f},
\end{equation}
applies and the center of mass will move locally at costant velocity if the observation time is short compared to the orbital period, thus making negligible its acceleration.\\Hereafter, by analogy with the previous notation, we identify $M_2\equiv m_f$ and $m_*\equiv m_t$.

\paragraph{Binary hardening.}
When the star is outside the secondary BH orbit, the Keplerian velocity profile imposes $\|\mathbf{v_f}(-\infty) \| =\| \mathbf{v_{CM}}\| > \|\mathbf{v_t}(-\infty) \|$. Therefore, at point where $m_f$ surpasses $m_t$ and they are both located on the same radius, $\mathbf{v}(-\infty)$ is nearly antiparallel to $\mathbf{v_{CM}}$ and $\Delta\mathbf{v}_\parallel$ can be read as the projected vector along $\mathbf{v_{CM}}$ rather than $\mathbf{v}(-\infty)$. Taking advantage of Equation (\ref{slingshot negative scalar product}), it follows that
\begin{equation}
\label{stellar_hardening_inequality}
    0<\langle \Delta\mathbf{v}_\parallel, \mathbf{v_{CM}} \rangle=\langle \Delta\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v_{CM}}\rangle=\Delta E_t/\mu.
\end{equation}
Equation (\ref{stellar_hardening_inequality}) shows that in the reference system centered on $M_1$ the star gains orbital energy and is scattered onto a wider orbit. On the other hand, the secondary BH experiences an equal and opposite change in orbital energy, leading to a reduction of the binary separation and, consequently, to binary hardening.\\ It is worth noticing that if $\Delta E_t>|E_t(-\infty)|$, which occurs at very high circular velocity of the binary BHs, stellar ejection occurs; in this regime ‘‘hypervelocity stars’’ (HVSs) travelling along nearly radial orbits are produced (see, e.g., \citealt{Quinlan_1996}; \citealt{Sesana_2006}).

\paragraph{Binary softening.}
Contrary to the previous case, binary softening takes place when the star is inside the secondary BH orbit. Now $\mathbf{v}(-\infty)$ is nearly parallel to $\mathbf{v_{CM}}$ at the crossover point and by repeating the same arguments used above it follows
\begin{equation}
     E_t/\mu=\langle \Delta\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v_{CM}}\rangle=\langle \Delta\mathbf{v}_\parallel, \mathbf{v_{CM}} \rangle<0.
\end{equation}
On one hand the star loses energy to the binary, it is pushed inward and may undergo tidal disruption due to primary BH \parencite{Lightman_1977}; on the other hand the binary gains energy and its semimajor axis increases, resulting in binary softening.

\par\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent
Although our discussion has so far focused on a specific three-body scattering configuration, the results can be readily generalized by observing that the distinction between binary hardening and softening is determined by the relative magnitude of the BH binary's orbital velocity  and the velocity of the incoming loss-cone star.\\The binary BH embidded in an isotropic stellar background with costant velocity dispersion $\sigma$ is expected to undergo three-body scatterings with loss-cone stars that will approach the galactic centre where the binary lives with velocity of the order of $\sigma$. Because the specific binding energy of a Keplerian with reduced mass $\mu$ and semimajor axis $a$ is
\begin{equation}
 E_b=\frac{G\mu}{2a}
\end{equation}
and the specific energy of the loss-cone star is of the order of $\sigma^2$, the velocity criterion can be conveniently reformulated in terms of the specific energy. When $E_b\ll \sigma^2$, the binary is said to be soft, and repeated stellar encounters tend to further decrease its binding energy. On the other hand, when $E_b\gg \sigma^2$, the binary is hard, and stellar encounters, on average, increase its binding energy. This behavior is well summarized by Heggie's law (see, e.g., \textcite{Heggie_1975}; \textcite{Heggie_1993}.
\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Heggie's law. --}
\emph{On average, hard binaries get harder and soft binaries get
softer.}
\medskip

\noindent By extrapolation of the two limiting cases, one can define the "hardening radius" $a_h$ as the binary separation at which binary passes from the soft to the hard regime. Throughout this thesis we adopt the definition proposed by \textcite{Quinlan_1996}, unless otherwise specified:\begin{equation}\label{a_h definition}
 a_h=\frac{GM_2}{4\sigma^2}.
\end{equation}
At this stage, it should be clear that stellar scattering plays a fundamental role in driving the binary toward the regime in which GWs emission dominates its evolution. Nevertheless, one may wonder how a binary can evolve from the soft to the hard regime. The key point is that this transition is driven primarily by dynamical friction against the stellar background, rather than by individual stellar encounters, as discussed in \S~\ref{sec:Two-Body Relaxation and Dynamical Friction}.\\The discussion then turns to the loss-cone refilling problem, whose efficient replenishment is essential to ensure a sustained supply of low angular momentum stars capable of driving the continued hardening of the binary through stellar ejection.




















\section{Stellar Scattering and Loss Cone Problem}\label{sec:Loss Cone}
In \S~\ref{sec:Gravitational Slingshot Effect} we have been concerned in describing the phisics of three-body scattering, pointing out its relevance to the orbital decay of the BH binary. In the following Section, instead, we will be explaining which stars can effectively exchange energy and angular momentum with the binary, the rate at which these close encounters take place and how all of this enters the self consistent model for MBHBs evolution developed by \textcite{Quinlan_1996} and \textcite{Sesana_2010} that will be presented in Chapter \ref{chap:chapter2}.\\Let $a(t)$ be the binary semimajor axis. Numerical experiments demonstrated that only stars intersecting the binary orbit can efficiently extract energy and angular momentum from the binary when ejected at higher velocity \parencite{Mikkola_1992}. This class of stars of the stellar background populates the so-called “binary loss cone” and comprises both low-energy stars and high-energy, low angular momentum stars. Under the assumption of Keplerian orbits, the former have small mean radius $(\langle r\rangle<a)$ and specific energy $E_*<-GM/a$, while latter spend most of their orbital period $P(E_*)$ far from the binary but a close encounter can occur at the pericenter $r_P$ whenever $r_P<a$.\\The size of the binary loss cone, $J_{lc}$, in specific angular momentum space, hereafter J-space, is a function of $E_*$ and can be determined from the maximum and minimum angular momentum values allowed at a given specific energy.
 At the configuration of closest approach, the star radial velocity vanishes and the specific energy in terms of $r_P$ is
\begin{equation}\label{Pericenter stellar energy}
 E_*=\frac{J^2}{2r_P^2}-\frac{GM}{r_P}.
\end{equation}
From Equation (\ref{Pericenter stellar energy}) follows that the minimum value of $J$, below which the star enters the binary loss cone, is
\begin{equation}\label{loss cone definition}
    J_{lc}(E_*)\equiv \left[2\left(E_*+\frac{GM}{a}\right)\right]^{1/2}a,
\end{equation}
which can be approximated to $\sim \sqrt{2GMa}$ as the vast majority of stars orbit far from the binary and have a specific angular energy $|E_*|\ll GM/a$.\\Besides, stars of fixed specific energy $E_*$, other than a minimum of the specific angular momentum $J_{min}=J_{lc}(E_*)$, possess a maximum set by the chain of inequalities
\begin{equation}
\label{E-J loss cone inequality}
 E_*\ge \frac{J^2}{2r^2}-\frac{GM}{r}\equiv V_{eff}(r)\ge V_{eff,\,min}=-\frac{G^2M^2}{2J^2},
\end{equation}
holding because of the non-negative behavior of the radial kinetic term. This constrain poses an upper limit to $J$, that is
\begin{equation}
 J_{max}(E_*)=\frac{GM}{\sqrt{2|E_*|}},
\end{equation}
corresponding to the specific angular momentum of a circular orbit of specific energy $E_*$ \parencite{Capozziello_2008}.\\Once the binary has depleted, on a dynamical timescale $P(E_*)$, the population of stars that are bound to it and intersect its orbit—most of which, hence, reside within the binar sphere of influence—further hardening through stellar slingshot interactions depends on the rate at which the binary loss cone is replenished by stellar diffusion in both energy and angular momentum space, allowing new stars to enter the loss cone \parencite{Lightman_1977}. This diffusion arises from stellar encounters
that have been proven to be efficient in exchanging angular momentum and results in a random walk (RW) in J-space (see, e.g., \citealt{Milosavljevic_2003}; \citealt{Merritt_2013}). Determining the rate at which this process occurs is in order to understand how it enters the self-consistent hybrid model devoled by \textcite{Quinlan_1996} and \textcite{Sesana_2010}, discussed in chapter \ref{chap:chapter2}.\\To this end, let us define $j_2$ the rms angular momentum transferred in one orbital period  and $J_{max}(E_*)$ the same quantity associated with the timescale $t_{2b}$. Only stars with $J_{lc}\le J\le J_{lc}+j_2$ can be scattered into the loss cone over one orbital period and the kind of loss cone replenishing mechanism, along with the associated timescale, are set by the dimensionless quantity
\begin{equation}
    q(E_*)\equiv\frac{j_2^2}{J_{min}^2}.
\end{equation}
Taking Figure \ref{loss cone image} as a reference, $q(E_*)$ enables us to distinguish between two extreme scenarios:
\paragraph{Empty Loss Cone.}
This condition is represented by picture on the left in Figure \ref{loss cone image}(b).\\When $q(E_*)\ll1$ the RW step size is very small compared to the loss cone size, making the process ascribable to a diffusion problem with timescale $t_{2b}$. Therefore, stars are removed from the loss cone faster than the rate at which it is fed, hence, remeining nearly empty.

\paragraph{Full Loss Cone.}
This condition is represented by the picture on the right in Figure \ref{loss cone image}(b).\\The loss cone is smaller than the RW step size and even high $J$ stars can be scattered into the loss cone by many, weak stellar encounters taking place during one robital period. Thus, the rate at which loss cone stars are expelled is lower than the rate of refilling, causing the loss cone to remain almost full.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{Loss_cone.png}
    \caption{Panel (a): Velocity distribution of stars with fixed energy $E_*\equiv E$ and angular momentum $J$. The latter varies accordingly to Equation (\ref{E-J loss cone inequality}). The vertical axis points towards the compact object of mass $M$.\\Panel (b): The velocity sphere viewed from below. On the left the empty loss cone scenario. On the right the full loss cone regime. Figure taken from \textcite{Lightman_1977}.}
    \label{loss cone image}
\end{figure}
By employng the same dimensional analysis carried out by \textcite{Lightman_1977}, we expect that the stellar capture rate per unit energy $\Gamma_E(E_*)$ could be written as
\begin{equation}\label{Lightman lc rate}
 \Gamma_E(E_*)\equiv\frac{d\Gamma}{dE_*}\approx \epsilon|E_*|^{-1}F_{max}(E_*)
\end{equation}
where $F_{max}(E_*)\sim N(E_*)/P(E_*)$ denotes the maximum rate of loss cone replenishing from stars with specific energy $E_*$ and $\epsilon$ is a correction factor helping us to account for the fraction of stars within the loss cone.\\Owing to the 2-D nature of the loss-cone problem in the J-space, $\epsilon$ is expected to be an area correction factor, thus scaling as
\begin{equation}
 \epsilon \sim \frac{J_{lc}^2}{(J_{lc}+j_2)^2}.
\end{equation}
Therefore, in the full loss cone regime, where $J_{lc}\ll j_2\approx J_{max}$, one easily finds that
$\sim J_{lc}^2/J_{max}^2\sim a/r$ and, by substitution into Equation (\ref{Lightman lc rate}),
\begin{equation}\label{stellar flux per unit energy}
 \Gamma_E(E_*)\approx |E_*|^{-1}\frac{a}{r}\frac{N(E_*)}{P(E_*)}.
\end{equation}
Equation (\ref{stellar flux per unit energy}) allows us to finally discuss how loss-cone refilling is accounted for in the Quinlan–Sesana hybrid model.\\In his scattering experiments, \textcite{Quinlan_1996} quantified the binary dimensioness hardening rate $H$ and eccentricity growth rate $K$ through the flux of stars
\begin{equation}\label{Quinlan Gamma_Q}
\Gamma_Q=\left(\frac{\rho}{m_*}\right)\Sigma v
\end{equation}
across the binary effective cross section $\Sigma$, where $\rho$ is the stellar mass density, $m_*$ is the stellar mass, and $v$ is the stellar velocity. Let $b$ denote the impact parameter of a star initially unbound to the binary of mass $M$ and undergoing a three-body interaction with it. The corresponding interaction cross section can then be defined as
\begin{equation}\label{binary cross section}
\Sigma \equiv \pi b_{\rm max}^2,
\end{equation}
where $b_{\rm max}$ is the maximum impact parameter for which stellar capture occurs. If we assume this to take place whenever $r_p<a$,
mindful of the gravitational focusing
\begin{equation}\label{gravitational focusing}
 b^2=\frac{2GMr_P}{v^2}
\end{equation}
relating impact parameter and pericenter distance, then we can pose $b_{max}=b(a)$ and
express $\Gamma_Q$ in Equation (\ref{Quinlan Gamma_Q}) as
\begin{equation}
 \Gamma_Q=2\pi \left(\frac{M}{m_*}\right) \frac{G\rho a}{v},
\end{equation}
with $\rho$ standing for the mass density of the homogeneous stellar background.\\On the other hand, \textcite{Sesana_2010} estimated the rate $\Gamma$ in Equation (\ref{Lightman lc rate}) under the assumption of a full loss cone, adopting an isothermal stellar sphere of total mass $M_*$, influence radius $r_{\rm inf}$ and costant stellar velocity dispersion $\sigma$.\\If all stars have the same mass $m_*$, the total number of stars inside $r$ is simply proportional to the stellar mass enclosed within this sphere, that is
\begin{equation}
 N(<r)=\frac{M}{m_*}\frac{r}{r_{inf}},
\end{equation}
while, under the assumption of Keplerian orbits bound to the binary, the energy of each star depends on the semimajor axis only through the relation
\begin{equation}
 E_*(r)=-\frac{GMm_*}{2r}.
\end{equation}
In this regime, by exploiting the relation $dE_=|E_|,d\ln r$, we can rewrite Equation (\ref{stellar flux per unit energy}) as (see, e.g., \textcite{Perets_2007}; \textcite{Perets_2008})
\begin{equation}\label{Sesana differential Gamma}
\frac{d\Gamma}{d\log r}=\frac{a}{r}\frac{N(<r)}{P(r)}.
\end{equation}
Finally, approximating the orbital period as $P(r)\sim r/\sigma$ and substituting the estimates for $N(<r)$ and $P(r)$ derived above into Equation (\ref{Sesana differential Gamma}), integration yields
\begin{equation}\label{Sesana Gamma}
\Gamma=2\pi \frac{M}{m_*}\frac{G\rho_{\rm inf}a}{\sigma}.
\end{equation}
A comparison between Equations (\ref{Quinlan Gamma_Q})  and (\ref{Sesana Gamma}) shows that, by associating the velocity $v$ of an unbound star at infinity with the velocity dispersion $\sigma$ of the isothermal sphere, the prescriptions adopted for $H$ and $K$ effectively describe a system in which the loss cone is full at $r_{\rm inf}$. However, by introducing the dimensioless factor $LCN$, it is possible to describe, within the hybrid model formulation, different regimes of the loss-cone simply by replacing $\rho_{inf}$ with $LCN\cdot \rho_{inf}$ as it will be shown in chapter \ref{chap:chapter3}.













\section{Gravitational Radiation from Black Hole Binaries}
\label{sec:Gravitational Radiation from Black Hole Binaries}




\subsection{Linearized Gravitational Field Equation}

The starting point of the theoretical derivation of GWs properties, such as the energy and angular momentum they transport, from BH binary and the associated back-reaction on the astrophysical source, is the linearization of  field equations of Generl Relativity (GR)
\begin{equation}\label{Einstein field eq.}
    R_{\mu \nu}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu \nu} R=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}T_{\mu \nu}.
\end{equation}
These constitute ten coupled, non-linear, second-order partial differential equations for the metric tensor; neverthless, they are not sufficient to uniquely set the ten indipendent components of $g_{\mu\nu}$ due to the four  Bianchi identities, equivalent to the same number of differential constraints, resulting in six  independent equations only. These four degrees of freedom are intimately connected to gauge symmetries, reflecting the arbitrariness of the four coordinates $x^\mu$ used to represent and solve Equation (\ref{Einstein field eq.}).\\To show how gravitational radiation emerges from Equation (\ref{Einstein field eq.}), we need to expand it around the Minkowski metric $\eta_{\mu \nu}=diag(-1,+1,+1,+1)$; therefore we assume
\begin{equation}
    g_{\mu \nu}(x)=\eta_{\mu \nu}+h_{\mu \nu}(x),
\end{equation}
with $|h_{\mu \nu}|\ll1$ being a symmetric tensor representing a small perturbation of the flat spacetime.\\The left-hand side of Equation (\ref{Einstein field eq.}) encodes the geometry and curvature of spacetime and contains all terms that are functions of the metric; in terms of the affine connection
\begin{equation}\label{affine connection def}
    \Gamma^\lambda _{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}g^{\lambda \rho}(\partial_\mu g_{\nu\rho}+\partial_\nu g_{\rho\mu}-\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu})
\end{equation}
and the Riemann tensor
\begin{equation}\label{Riemmann tensor def}
    R_{\lambda\mu}{}^{\rho}{}_{\nu}=\partial_\lambda \Gamma_{\mu\nu}^\rho-\partial_\mu \Gamma_{\lambda\nu}^\rho+\Gamma_{\lambda\sigma}^\rho \Gamma_{\mu\nu}^\sigma-\Gamma_{\mu\sigma}^\rho \Gamma_{\lambda\nu}^\sigma,
\end{equation}
they can be expressed indeed as 
\begin{equation}\label{Ricci tensor def}
    R_{\mu\nu}=R_{\sigma\mu}{}^{\sigma}{}_{\nu}
\end{equation}
and 
\begin{equation}\label{Ricci scalar def} R=g^{\alpha\beta}R_{\alpha\beta}=R_\sigma^\sigma.
\end{equation}
On the right-hand side, instead, appears the Energy-Momentum tensor; it can be read as the source term of the gravitational field equations and turns out to be
\begin{equation}\label{Energy-Momentum tensor def}
    T^{\mu\nu}=(\rho+p/c^2)u^\mu u^\nu+pg^{\mu\nu}
\end{equation}
for a perfect fluid of density $\rho$, pressure $p$ and four-velocity $v^\mu$.\\It straightforward to prove that, to the first order in the metric perturbation, they become:
\begin{equation}\label{linearized Ricci tensor}
    R_{\mu \nu}=-\frac{1}{2}(\Box h_{\mu \nu}+\partial_\mu \partial_\nu h-\partial_\lambda\partial_\mu h_\nu^\lambda-\partial_\nu\partial_\lambda h_\mu^\lambda)
\end{equation}
 and
\begin{equation}\label{linearized ricci scalar}
    R=\partial_\mu \partial_\nu h^{\mu\nu}-\Box h,
\end{equation}
where $h$ denotes $h_{\mu \nu}$ trace.\\Defining the so-called "trace reverse" of $h_{\mu\nu}$
\begin{equation}\label{trace reverse definition}
    \bar{h}_{\mu\nu}\equiv h_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}\eta_{\mu\nu} h
\end{equation}
and plugging the terms of Equations (\ref{linearized Ricci tensor}) and (\ref{linearized ricci scalar}) into Equation (\ref{Einstein field eq.}), the field equations take the form
\begin{equation}\label{linearized Einstein Eq. LONG}
    \Box \bar{h}_{\mu\nu} + \eta_{\mu\nu}\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta \bar{h}^{\alpha\beta}-\partial_\mu\partial_\lambda \bar{h}^\lambda_\nu-\partial_\nu\partial_\lambda \bar{h}^\lambda_\mu=-\frac{16\pi G}{c^4}T_{\mu\nu}.
\end{equation}
Taking advantage of the gauge freedom of Equation (\ref{Einstein field eq.}), it can be performed an infintesimal coordinate transformation of the kind
\begin{equation}
    x^\mu \rightarrow{}x'^\mu=x^\mu+\xi^\mu(x);
\end{equation}
as tensors obey to precise law of transformation, this yields
\begin{equation}
    \bar{h}'_{\mu\nu}=\frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\mu}\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\bar{h}_{\alpha\beta}\approx \bar{h}_{\mu\nu}-\partial _\mu \xi_\nu -\partial_\nu \xi_\mu+\eta_{\mu\nu}\partial_\sigma\xi^\sigma.
\end{equation}
Yet, if the four functions $\xi^\mu$ are chosen so as to satisfy the so-called De Donder gauge condition, i.e. $\Box\xi^\mu=\partial_\sigma\bar{h}^{\sigma\mu}$, then $\partial_\sigma\bar{h}'^{\sigma\mu}=0$ and, dropping the prime, Equation (\ref{linearized Einstein Eq. LONG}) semplifies to
\begin{equation}\label{linearized Einstein eq + gauge condition}
\begin{cases}
\Box \bar{h}_{\mu\nu} = -\dfrac{16\pi G}{c^4} T_{\mu\nu} \\
\partial_\mu\bar{h}^{\mu\nu}=0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
We are now ready to address the problem of determining  the general solution of the linearized Einstein's equations coupled with the De Donder gauge condition. 


\subsection{Generation and Propagation of Gravitational Waves}

To study the formation of GWs we initially focus on the solution of the problem defined in Equation (\ref{linearized Einstein eq + gauge condition}) inside the source where $T_{\mu\nu}\ne 0$.\\Linear partial differential equations solution can be obtained by the method of Green's function; by definition, the Green's function $G$ is the solution of the associated equation in case of a pont-like source, i.e.
\begin{equation}\label{Green equation}
    \Box_x G(x-x') = \delta^{(4)}(x-x'),
\end{equation}
with $\delta^{(4)}(x-x')$ being the four-dimensional Dirac function.\\It is relevant to note that, apart from the B.C. of the problem, $G$ depends only on the linear operator; therefore, from the radiation problem in electromagnetism, we already know that the proper solution is the retarded Green's function 
\begin{equation}\label{retarded Green function}
    G(x-x')=-\frac{1}{4\pi |\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x'}|}\delta^{(4)}(ct_{ret}-ct'),
\end{equation}
where 
\begin{equation}\label{retarded time def}
    t_{ret}=t-\frac{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x'}|}{c}
\end{equation}
is the time in past at which the radiation was emitted at $\mathbf{x'}$ in order to be observed at $\mathbf{x}$ at time $t$. Once the Green's function is found, the general solution will be the convolution between it and source term:
\begin{equation}\label{h_mn general solution}
    \bar{h}_{\mu\nu}(t,\mathbf{x})=\frac{4G}{c^4}\int d^3x'\frac{T_{\mu\nu}(t_{ret},\mathbf{x'})}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x'}|}.
\end{equation}
Prior to examining the formula reported in Equation (\ref{h_mn general solution}) and assessing which additional simplifications can be implemented and when they apply, it is appropriate to analyze how GWs propagate in vacuo.\\Outside the GWs source, $T_{\mu\nu}$ vanishes and, owing to the linearity of the d’Alembert operator, the solution can be expressed as superposition of plane waves without loss of generality, that is
\begin{equation}
    \bar{h}_{\mu\nu}(x)=\int d^3k (A_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{k})e^{ik_\rho x^\rho}+c.c.)
\end{equation}
However, not all plane waves satisfy the linearized field equation in vacuo along with the gauge condition but linearity enables us to study the properties of a single plane wave separately; consequently, inserting the Ansatz $\bar{h}_{\mu\nu}\sim A_{\mu\nu}e^{ik_\rho x^\rho}$ yields the following two constraints:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $(k_\rho k^\rho=0)$: the four-vector $k^\mu=(\omega/c,\mathbf{k})$ is a light-like vector (GWs travel at light speed $c$ in vacuo).
    \item $(k_\mu A^{\mu\nu}=0)$: it implies $A^{0\nu}=A^{3\nu}$ if we deal with GWs propagating along the $x^3$ direction, hence $k^\mu=(k,0,0,k)$.
\end{enumerate}
At this stage, the amplitude tensor $A^{\mu\nu}$ possesses six indipendet components --- ten, due to symmetry, reduced by the four constraints imposed by the gauge conditions --- and can be written as
\begin{equation}\label{A^mn 6 dof}   
A^{\mu\nu} =
\begin{pmatrix}
A^{00} & A^{10} & A^{20} & A^{00} \\
A^{10} & A^{11} & A^{12} & A^{10} \\
A^{20} & A^{12} & A^{22} & A^{20} \\
A^{00} & A^{10} & A^{20} & A^{00}
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Out of the six polarizations shown in Equation (\ref{A^mn 6 dof}), four are unphysical because they vanish when the residual gauge freedom associated with four arbitrary harmonic functions $\xi^\mu$ is exploited. One is free to adopt functions proportional to the plane wave solutions of the vacuo problem, i.e. $\xi^\mu=\epsilon^\mu e^{ik_\rho x^\rho}$ are harmonic, and to set their amplitudes $\epsilon^\mu$ such that 
\begin{equation}\label{A^mn TT gauge}   
A^{\mu\nu}_{TT} =
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & h_+ & h_x & 0 \\
0 & h_x & -h_+ & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The coordinate system where $A^{\mu\nu}$ is in the form of Equation (\ref{A^mn TT gauge}), with two indipendent polarizations only, and defined by 
\begin{equation}
    \partial_\mu h^{\mu\nu}=0, \quad h^{0\mu}=0, \quad h^i_i=0,
\end{equation}
is referred to as "TT gauge"\footnote{In the TT gauge, according to the definition provided, $\bar{h}_{ij}^{TT}=h_{ij}^{TT}$.}.\\Having analyzed the propagation of GWs in vacuo and the properties of the TT gauge, we are now in the position to focus again on the source solution reported in Equation (\ref{h_mn general solution}). One of the greatest simplifications to this integral consists of the so-called "Compact-source approximation"; it applies outside the source whenever its size is negligible with respect to the source-detector separation. In this regime, we can retain the leading term of the multipole expansion only, thus
\begin{equation}\label{h_mn compact source solution}
    h_{ij}^{TT}(t,\mathbf{x})=\frac{4G}{c^4r}\int d^3x'T_{ij}(t_{ret},\mathbf{x'}).
\end{equation}
Furthermore, far from the source, the conservation of the Energy-Momentum tensor, namely $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}\approx\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=0$, yields
\begin{equation}\label{h_mn final form}
    h_{ij}^{TT}(t,\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{2G}{rc^4}\ddot{Q}^{TT}_{ij}(t-r/c)
\end{equation}
at lowest order in the low-velocity expansion.\\The term $Q^{TT}_{ij}$ introduced in Equation (\ref{h_mn final form})\footnote{The components with at least one time index are excluded from our analysis because they are constant and therefore do not contribute to GWs formation.} is the representation of the quadrupole moment in the class of coordinate systems satisfying the TT gauge conditions; defining the quadrupole moment of $T^{00}/c^2=\rho$ as
\begin{equation}\label{M_ij definition}
    M^{ij}\equiv \frac{1}{c^2}\int d^3x T^{00}(t,\boldsymbol{x})x^ix^j,
\end{equation}
it is thus possible to write
\begin{equation}\label{Q_ij def}
    Q^{ij}\equiv M^{ij}-\frac{1}{3}\delta^{ij}M_{kk}=\int d^3x\rho(t,\mathbf{x})\left(x^ix^j-\frac{1}{3}r^2\delta^{ij}\right).
\end{equation}
Equation (\ref{h_mn compact source solution}) shows that, unlike electromagnetic radiation, at lowest order the gravitational radiation arises from changes in the quadrupole moment; other important observables such as the energy and angular momentum carried by GWs, needed to estimate the back-reaction, will be derived from the quantities defined in Equation (\ref{Q_ij def}) indeed.\\The energy and angular momentum conservation laws impose that the rates a which these quantities are carried away by GWs at time $t$ and at large separation $r$ from the source coincide with the corresponding rates of orbital energy ---if the emitting system consists of a BH binary--- and angular momentum, negletting the spin contribute, loss evaluated at the appropriate retarded time; in formulae:
\begin{equation}\label{energy loss rate by GWs}
    P_{quad}=\frac{dE_{gw}}{dt}=\frac{G}{5c^5}\langle\dddot{Q}_{ij}\dddot{Q}_{ij}\rangle=-\frac{dE_{source}}{dt},
\end{equation}
or equivalently in terms of $M_{ij}$
\begin{equation}\label{P_quad in terms of M_ij}
     P_{quad}=\frac{G}{5c^5}\left\langle\dddot{M}_{ij}\dddot{M}_{ij}-\frac{1}{3}(\dddot{M}_{kk})^2\right\rangle,
\end{equation}
and 
\begin{equation}\label{angular momentum loss rate by GWs}
    \frac{dL^i_{gw}}{dt}=\frac{2G}{5c^5}\epsilon^{ijk}\langle\ddot{Q}_{jl}\dddot{Q}_{kl}\rangle=-\frac{dL^i_{source}}{dt}.
\end{equation}
Equations (\ref{energy loss rate by GWs}) and (\ref{angular momentum loss rate by GWs}) will be of fundamental importance for estimating the back-reaction induced by GWs emission on the black hole binary.















\subsection{Gravitational Waves from Black Hole Binaries and Back-Reaction}
We now focus on the gravitational radiation emitted by two masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ moving along elliptic Keplerian orbit. Let $m=m_1+m_2$ and $\mu=m_1\cdot m_2/{m}$ be the total mass and the reduced mass respectively.\\Regardless of whether the orbit is bound or unbound, the motion of the two bodies takes place in a plane due to the conservation of the angular momentum $\mathbf{L}$; therefore, in this plane, the x-y plane, perpendicular to $\mathbf{L}$, it is convenient to  
introduce the polar coordinates $(r,\psi)$ with origin at center-of-mass.\\In this frame, as before denoted by $S_{CM}$, 
\begin{equation}\label{L of two body problem}
    L_z=\mu r^2\dot{\psi}
\end{equation}
coincides with the modulus $L$ of the costant vector while, as a consequence of the Köenig theorem,
\begin{equation}\label{E of two body problem}
    E=\frac{1}{2}\mu \dot{r}^2+\frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}-\frac{Gm\mu}{r}
\end{equation}
exhibits only the term of kinetic energy corresponding to the relative motion of the system, as in $S_{CM}$ holds $K_{CM}=0$ by definition.\\The integration of Equation (\ref{E of two body problem}) requires, as in \parencite{Capozziello_2008}, the introduction of the auxiliary variable $w(\psi)=1/r(\psi)$; plugging it into the energy conservation identity, Equation (\ref{E of two body problem}) becomes 
\begin{equation}
    w'^2+w^2-\frac{2Gm_1m_2\mu}{L^2}w=\frac{2\mu E}{L^2}
\end{equation}
where $w'\equiv dw/d\psi$. Differentiating both sides of the last equation with respect to $\psi$ leads to
\begin{equation}\label{u differential equation}
    w'\left(w''+w-\frac{Gm_1m_2\mu}{L^2}\right)=0.
\end{equation}
Apart from the solution $w=const$, corresponding to a circular binary, the problem admits harmonic–oscillator–like and, upon solving for $r(\psi)$, it is found to be given by
\begin{equation}\label{two-body solution}
    r(\psi)=\frac{R}{1+e\cos{\psi}},
\end{equation}
with $R=L^2/(Gm\mu^2)$ being semi-latus rectum --- the polar axis is aligned to the major axis $2a$, so that 
$R$ corresponds to the distance between the origin and the point of the conic at $\psi=\pi/2$--- and is related to the eccentricity $e$ by 
\begin{equation}\label{eccentricity formula}
    e^2=1-\frac{R}{a}=1+\frac{2EL^2}{G^2m^2\mu^3}.
\end{equation}
It is worth of noticing that we are interested in the GWs emission process by a BH binary, which is by definition a gravitationally bound system; therefore, from this point onward it will be assumed $E<0$ and thus, by virtue of Equation (\ref{eccentricity formula}), $0\le e<1$.
Since the two semiaxes are given by
\begin{equation}\label{a and b as a function of e}
    a=\frac{R}{1-e^2} \qquad \qquad b=\frac{R}{\sqrt{1-e^2}},
\end{equation}
inserting Equation (\ref{eccentricity formula}) into the first identity of Equation (\ref{a and b as a function of e}) gives the semi-major axis in terms of $E$; explicitly:
\begin{equation}\label{a as a function of E}
    a=-\frac{Gm\mu}{2E}
\end{equation}
It follows that fixing the energy means setting the semi-major axis.\\The functions $r(t)$ and $\psi(t)$ can be obtained by direct integration of Equations (\ref{L of two body problem}) and (\ref{E of two body problem}); they are usually given in the parametric form
\begin{equation}
    r(t)=a[1-e\cos{u(t)}] \qquad \qquad\psi(t)=\frac{\cos{u(t)}-e}{1-e\cos{u(t)}}
\end{equation}
in terms of the "eccentric anomaly" $u$ defined in the famous Kepler equation
\begin{equation}
    u(t)-e\sin{u(t)}=\left(\frac{Gm}{a^3}\right)^{1/2}t\equiv \omega_0 t.
\end{equation}
The transformation from from polar cordinates $(r,\psi)$ to Cartesian ones $(x,y)$ is illistrated below
\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
x = r \cos\psi = a\left[\cos u(t) - e\right] \\
y = r \sin\psi = b \sin u(t) .
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
We now possess all the necessary ingredients to proceed with the computation of the rates of energy and angular momentum carried by GWs, which will play a fundamental role in assessing the back-reaction on the binary system.\\In the reference system $S_{CM}$ holds $\rho(t,\mathbf{x})=\mu\delta(x-r\cos{\psi})\delta(y-r\sin{\psi})\delta(z)$
and, according to \\Equation (\ref{M_ij definition}), it is immediate to get
\begin{equation}
M_{ij} = \mu r^2(t)\begin{bmatrix}
\cos^2(\psi) & \sin{(\psi)\cos(\psi)} \\
\sin{(\psi)\cos(\psi)} & \sin^2(\psi)
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The time derivatives are easier computed when $r$, which is a function of time itself, is expressed as a function of $\psi$; this is accomplished by replacing this variable with the general solution of the two-body problem provided by Equation (\ref{two-body solution}). A simple computation returns the third time derivatives needed to derive the power radiated in the quadrupole approximation, i.e.
\begin{equation}\label{d3M _11/dt3}  
\dddot{M}_{11} = \beta (1 + e \cos \psi)^2 \left[ 2 \sin 2\psi + 3e \sin \psi \cos^2 \psi \right],
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{d3M _22/dt3}  
    \dddot{M}_{22} = \beta (1 + e \cos \psi)^2 \left[ -2 \sin 2\psi - e \sin \psi (1 + 3 \cos^2 \psi) \right],
\end{equation}


\begin{equation}
    \dddot{M}_{12} = \beta (1 + e \cos \psi)^2 \left[ -2 \cos 2\psi + e \cos \psi (1 - 3 \cos^2 \psi) \right],
\end{equation}
where the factor $\beta$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
\beta^2 \equiv \frac{4 G^3 \mu^2 m^3}{a^5 (1 - e^2)^5}. 
\end{equation}
By means of Equation (\ref{energy loss rate by GWs}) we get in conclusion
\begin{equation}
    P(\psi)=\frac{8G^4}{15c^5}\frac{\mu^2m^3}{a^5(1-e^2)^5}(1+e\cos(\psi))^4[12(1+e\cos(\psi))^2+e^2\sin^2(\psi)].
\end{equation}
However, we recall that this istantaneous quantity is not constant over one orbital period for elliptic Keplerian orbits as in general $M_{ij}$ will suffer from different rate of variation from pericenter to apocenter; therefore, taking the temporal average over one orbital period, $T\equiv2\pi/\omega_0$ returns
\begin{equation}\label{P of GWs binary}
    P\equiv\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T dtP(\psi)=\frac{\omega_0}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} d\psi \frac{P(\psi)}{\dot{\psi}}=\frac{32G^4\mu^2m^3}{5c^5a^5}F(e),
\end{equation}
with 
\begin{equation}\label{F(e) definition}
    F(e)=\frac{1}{(1-e^2)^{7/2}}\left(1+\frac{73}{24}e^2+\frac{37}{96}e^4\right)
\end{equation}
being a function strongly depending on the eccentricity, which reduces to unity in case of circular binary.\\Throughout the inspiral phase, the binary radiates not only energy but angular momentum as well, carried by GWS, and undergoes secular changes in both semimajor axis and ellipticity until coalescence eventually occurs. Therefore, another key ingredient in evaluating the back-reaction on the binary evolution is the determination of the angular momentum loss rate. It is quantified in Equation (\ref{angular momentum loss rate by GWs}) which becomes
\begin{equation}\label{angular momentum loss rate in terms of M_ij}
    \frac{dL^i_{source}}{dt}=-\frac{2G}{5c^5}\epsilon^{ikl}\left<\dddot{M}_{ka}\dddot{M}_{la}\right>
\end{equation}
as the the terms of the form $\epsilon^{ijk}\delta_{jl}\dddot{Q}_{kl}$ vanish, since they correspond to the contraction of an antisymmetric tensor with a symmetric one.\\Taking into account that, in $S_{CM}$, all components $M_{i3}$ are identically zero and that $L=L_z=L^3_{source}$, one can rewrite Equation (\ref{angular momentum loss rate in terms of M_ij}) in a more convenient form consistent with the previous arguments:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dL}{dt}=\frac{4G}{5c^5}\langle \ddot{M}_{12}(\dddot{M}_{11}-\dddot{M}_{22})\rangle.
\end{equation}
The last identity provides $dL/dt$ in terms of the second and third derivatives of the quadrupole moment of $\rho$; although the second derivative $\ddot{M}_{12}$ has not been computed explicitly yet, it follows straightforwardly from the same procedure used to derive $\dddot{M}_{ij}$, hence
\begin{equation}\label{d2 M_12/dt2}
\ddot{M}_{12}
=
\frac{G \mu m}{a(1 - e^2)} \sin \psi
\left[
-4(1 + e \cos \psi)^2 \cos \psi
+ 2e \left( 3 \cos^2 \psi - 1 + 2e \cos^3 \psi \right)
\right].
\end{equation}
Yet, adopting the expressions for reported in Equations (\ref{d3M _11/dt3}), (\ref{d3M _22/dt3}) and (\ref{d2 M_12/dt2}) respectively, we get the angular momentum loss, corresponding to minus the angular momentum carried by the gravitational radiation in the quadrupole approximation; then, the temporal average over a single orbital period $T$, transformed into an integral over the angular position $\psi$, returns
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dL}{dt}=-\frac{32}{5}\frac{G^{7/2}\mu^2m^{5/2}}{c^5a^{7/2}}\frac{1}{(1-e^2)^2}\left(1+\frac{7}{8}e^2\right)
\end{equation}
Lastly, the simi-major axis and eccentricity evolution of the BH binary results by imposing the conservation of the energy and angular momentum of the system composed of the binary and GWs; differentiating Equations (\ref{eccentricity formula}) and (\ref{a as a function of E}) with respect to the time yields
\begin{equation}
    \frac{da}{dt}=\frac{Gm\mu}{2E^2(a)}(-P)
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    2e\frac{de}{dt}=\frac{2}{G^2m^2\mu^3}\left[-PL^2(a,e)+2E(a)L(a,e)\frac{dL}{dt}\right].
\end{equation}
By rearranging all the terms and performing the appropriate substitutions, \textcite{Peters_1963} gave 
\begin{equation}\label{da/dt Peterson}
    \frac{da}{dt} = -\frac{64}{5} \frac{G^3 \mu m^2}{c^5 a^3} \frac{1}{(1 - e^2)^{7/2}} \left( 1 + \frac{73}{24}e^2 + \frac{37}{96}e^4 \right)
\end{equation}
along with
\begin{equation}\label{de/dt Peterson}
    \frac{de}{dt} = -\frac{304}{15} \frac{G^3 \mu m^2}{c^5 a^4}  \frac{e}{(1 - e^2)^{5/2}} \left( 1 + \frac{121}{304}e^2 \right)
\end{equation}
that has been employed by \textcite{Quinlan_1996} and \textcite{Sesana_2010} in order to reconstruct evolutionary tracks of BH binaries through a "Hybrid model", which will be described later.\\Equations (\ref{da/dt Peterson}) and (\ref{de/dt Peterson}) prove that GWs emission  is efficient, more precisely at little binary separation due to $a^{-3}$ and $a^{-4}$ dependence respectively, not only in shrinking the BHs pair, by extracting orbital energy, but in promoting the orbit circularization as well, thus making this mechanism dominant over stellar scattering at late stage of binary inspiral.\\Furthermore, another key piece of information that can be derived from the theory is the expected coalescence time $\tau$ of the binary in the case of gravitational-wave emission alone. This should reproduce the characteristic hardening timescale of the final stage, when orbital shrinking driven by gravitational radiation takes over stellar scattering. This is obtained by combining Equations (\ref{da/dt Peterson}) and (\ref{de/dt Peterson}); this mathematical operation leads to a differential equation for $a=a(e)$, which reads
\begin{equation}\label{da/de Maggiore}
    \frac{da}{de}=\frac{12}{19}\frac{1+\frac{73}{24}e^2+\frac{37}{96}e^4}{e(1-e^2)\left(1+\frac{121}{304}e^2\right)}.
\end{equation}
The last equation admits analytic solution of the kind
\begin{equation}\label{a=a(e) Maggiore}
    a(e)=a_0\frac{g(e)}{g(e_0)}
\end{equation}
where 
\begin{equation}\label{g(e) Maggiore}
    g(e)=\frac{e^{12/19}}{1-e^2}\left(1+\frac{121}{304}e^2\right)^{870/2299}
\end{equation}
and $(a_0,e_0)$ denotes the initial point of the of binary evolutionary track.\\Last but not least, given the BH binary initial condition $(a_0,e_0)$, the coalescence time is found by integration over the eccentricity and by noticing that, if we set $(a(0),e(0))=(a_0,e_0)$, $e(\tau)=0$ due to orbit circularization:
\begin{equation}\label{tau coal. analytical}
\tau(a_0,e_0)=\int_0^{\tau(a_0,e_0)}dt=\int_{e_0}^{0}de \frac{dt}{de}=\int_{e_0}^{0}de \frac{a^4(e)(1-e^2)^{5/2}}{e\left(1+\frac{121}{304}e^2\right)}.
\end{equation}
By substituting Equation (\ref{a=a(e) Maggiore}) into Equation (\ref{tau coal. analytical}) one gets the final expression
\begin{equation}\label{t_coal Maggiore} 
\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}(a_0,e_0)
=
\frac{12}{19}
\frac{c^5}{G^3}
\frac{a_0^4}{\mu m^2}
\int_{0}^{e_0}
\frac{e^{29/19}
\left(1+\frac{121}{304}e^2\right)^{1181/2299}}
{(1-e^2)^{3/2}}
\,\mathrm{d}e.
\end{equation}

\section{Quinlan-Sesana Hybrid Model}
\label{sec:Quinlan-Sesana Hybrid Model}
As already discussed in Chapter \ref{chap:chapter1}, the coalescence time of a BH binary driven solely by GW emission depends strongly on several parameters, including the initial binary separation $a_0$ and eccentricity $e_0$. However, the GW-driven regime is reached only after a sequence of earlier hardening mechanisms, primarily dynamical friction and three-body stellar scattering, which dominate the early stages of the binary evolution. It is therefore essential to model the dynamical evolution of the binary by consistently accounting for the different hardening processes from its formation to final coalescence in order to obtain a reliable description of the binary evolutionary track, i.e. $a(t)$ and $e(t)$, and  its coalescence time.\\Although direct $N$-body simulations provide a self-consistent approach to carrying out this analysis, they remain computationally too demanding to efficiently explore the broad parameter space governing binary evolution. This parameter space, for our purposes, includes the BH masses, the binary separation and eccentricity at pairing, and the properties of the stellar background. In this context, the self-consistent hybrid model developed by \textcite{Quinlan_1996} and \textcite{Sesana_2010} provides an efficient alternative, overcoming the computational limitations and long runtimes associated with direct $N$-body experiments while retaining the key physical processes driving the binary evolution.\\Let us begin by introducing the Quinlan formalism and the main model parameters were determined through restricted three-body scattering experiment, eventually leading to the coupled differential equation for $(a(t),e(t))$. Adopting the original notation, we denote by $m_1$ and $m_2<m_1$ the primary and secondary BH masses, respectively, by $M_{12}=m_1+m_2$ the total binary mass, by $q=m_2/m_2$ the BHs mass ratio ,and by $m_*\ll m_2$ the mass of an individual star, assuming all stars have the same mass. In his work, \textcite{Quinlan_1996} studied the dynamical evolution of a BH binary embedded in a fixed, homogeneous background of unbound stars with density $\rho$ and a Maxwellian velocity distribution characterised by a constant velocity dispersion $\sigma$. The description relies on three dimensionless quantities, $H$, $K$, and $J$, which denote the dimensionless binary hardening rate, eccentricity growth rate, and stellar mass ejection rate, respectively. They are defined as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{H Q96}
    H=\frac{\sigma}{G\rho}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{a}\right),
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{K Q96}
    K=\frac{de}{d\ln({1/a})}
\end{equation}
and 
\begin{equation}\label{J Q96}
    J=\frac{1}{M_{12}}\frac{dM_{ej}}{d\ln({1/a})}.
\end{equation}
Let us focus on Equations (\ref{H Q96}) and (\ref{K Q96}). Both dimensioless rates and the derviatives of the binary parameters account for the various, indipendent mechanisms involved during its evolution, thus
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\frac{da}{dt} &= \sum_i \left.\frac{da}{dt}\right|_i
= -\frac{a^2 G \rho}{\sigma}\sum_i H_i \\
\frac{de}{dt} &= \sum_i \left.\frac{de}{dt}\right|_i
= -\frac{a G \rho}{\sigma}\sum_i H_iK_i
\end{aligned}
\right.
\label{Sesana_2010 a and e derivatives}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
&H_i=\frac{\sigma}{a^2G\rho} \left.\frac{da}{dt}\right|_i\\
&K_i= a \left.\frac{de}{dt}\right|_i
\left(\sum_j\left.\frac{da}{dt}\right|_j\right)^{-1}
\end{aligned}
\right.
\label{Sesana_2010 H_i and K_i}
\end{equation}
where the index $i$ identifies a precise dynamical process \parencite{Sesana_2010}.\\As in \textcite{Quinlan_1996} and \textcite{Sesana_2006}, in our numerical experiments, we account for the binary evolution due to close encounters with unbound, loss-cone stars and by GW emission, as described by the equations derived by \textcite{Peters_1963}. Therefore, we assume
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\frac{da}{dt} &= \left.\frac{da}{dt}\right|_u+\left.\frac{da}{dt}\right|_{GW}
= -\frac{a^2 G \rho}{\sigma}( H_u + H_{GW}) \\
\frac{de}{dt} &= \left.\frac{de}{dt}\right|_u+\left.\frac{de}{dt}\right|_{GW}
= -\frac{a G \rho}{\sigma}( H_uK_u+H_{GW}K_{GW})
\end{aligned}
\right.
\label{Sesana_2010 a and e derivatives for u and GW}
\end{equation}
The latter, derived in \S~\ref{sec:Gravitational Radiation from Black Hole Binaries}, is reported here for completeness and to ensure consistency with the notation adopted in this chapter:
\begin{equation}
    \left.\frac{da}{dt}\right|_{GW} = -\frac{64}{5} \frac{G^3 \mu m^2}{c^5 a^3} \frac{1}{(1 - e^2)^{7/2}} \left( 1 + \frac{73}{24}e^2 + \frac{37}{96}e^4 \right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \left.\frac{de}{dt}\right|_{GW} = -\frac{304}{15} \frac{G^3 \mu m^2}{c^5 a^4}  \frac{e}{(1 - e^2)^{5/2}} \left( 1 + \frac{121}{304}e^2 \right).
\end{equation}
The former, instead, was independently derived by \textcite{Quinlan_1996} and \textcite{Sesana_2006} using two different approaches, as shown below. Indeed, with the aim of deriving the dimensionless rates, both works employ suites of three-body scattering experiments designed to characterize the response of a BH binary to repeated close encounters with stars over its lifetime. However, while \textcite{Quinlan_1996} fixes the incoming stellar velocity $v$ and varies the binary separation $a$, \textcite{Sesana_2006} adopt the opposite approach, varying $v$ at fixed $a$. It is worth of noting that the two approaches are physically equivalent, but lead to different parametrizations of the functions $H_u$ and $K_u$. When numerically integrating Equations (\ref{Sesana_2010 a and e derivatives for u and GW}), the parametrization provided by \textcite{Sesana_2006} will be adopted, as it directly involves the quantities being integrated.\\Let us now briefly review how $H_u$ and $K_u$ were originally estimated. The underlying idea is to infer the variations in the binary orbital energy and angular momentum from the corresponding changes experienced by the scattered star, which are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign as a consequence of energy and angular momentum conservation. The first step is to define the dimensionless energy change
\begin{equation}\label{C quinlan}
    C\equiv\frac{a\Delta E_*}{G\mu},
\end{equation}
introduced by \textcite{Hills_1983}, and the dimensionless impact parameter
\begin{equation}
 x\equiv \frac{b}{b_0}
\end{equation}
with $b_0^2=2GM_{12}a/v^2$.\\Denoting by $\langle C\rangle$ the average over all angular variables, the “single-velocity” hardening rate $H_1(v)$, derived from restricted three-body scattering experiments in which all stars have the same velocity at infinity, is given by
\begin{equation}\label{H_1 Quinlan}
H_1(v)=\frac{1}{\pi b_0^2}\int db,(2\pi b) 4 \pi \langle C\rangle=8 \pi \int dx, \langle C\rangle x \equiv8\pi I_x(C).
\end{equation}
where the operator $I_x(C)$ is implicitly defined by Equation (\ref{H_1 Quinlan}). Finally, the hardening rate $H_u(\sigma)$ is obtained by averaging the single-velocity hardening rate $H_1(v)$ over the Maxwellian distribution of stellar velocities:
\begin{equation}
H_u(\sigma)=\int_0^\infty dv\, 4\pi v^2 H_1(v)\frac{\sigma}{v} f(v,\sigma)
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
f(v,\sigma)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{3/2}}
e^{-\frac{v^2}{2\sigma^2}}
\end{equation}
is the Maxwellian velocity distribution associated with the costant velocity dispersion $\sigma$.\\Similarly, one finds $K_u$ from the “single-velocity” dimensionless eccentricity growth rate $K_1$, which has the same meaning of $H_1$. It can be derived starting from the expression for the binary eccentricity,
\begin{equation}\label{Quinlan eccentricity formula}
e^2=1+\frac{2EL^2}{\mu(GM_{12})^2},
\end{equation}
which relates the eccentricity to the binary orbital energy $E$ and angular momentum $L$. Differentiating Equation (\ref{Quinlan eccentricity formula}) and expressing the resulting variations in terms of finite differences yields
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\Delta e}{\Delta ln(1/a)}=\frac{1-e^2}{2e}\left[-2\left(\frac{E}{\Delta E}\right)\left(\frac{\Delta L_z}{L_z}\right)-1\right]=\frac{1-e^2}{2e}(B-C),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{B quinlan}
    B\equiv-\frac{M_{12}}{m_*}\frac{\Delta L_z}{L_z}.
\end{equation}
is the dimensionless angular momentum change. Finally, by integrating over all possible impact parameters and angular variables and normalizing the result to $B$, the single-velocity eccentricity growth rate $K_1$ turns out to be
\begin{equation}
K_1=\frac{\int dx,xC(x)\left\langle \frac{\Delta e}{\Delta\ln(1/a)}\right\rangle}{\int dx,xC(x)}
=\frac{1-e^2}{2e}\left[\frac{I_x(B-C)}{I_x(C)}\right].
\end{equation}
The best-fitting functions found by \textcite{Sesana_2006} from numerical experiments to $H_u$ and $K_u$ are
\begin{equation}\label{H fit Sesana}
 H(a)=A_H\left(1+\frac{a}{a_{0,H}}\right)^{\gamma_H}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{K fit Sesana}
 K(a,e)=A_K\left(1+\frac{a}{a_{0,K}}\right)^{\gamma_K}+B_K,
\end{equation}
with best-fitting parameters listed in Tables \ref{tab:H best-fit parameters} and \ref{tab:K best-fit_parameters}.

In contrast, the dimensionless eccentricity growth rate $K_u$ depends on both the binary eccentricity $e$ and the separation $a$. Following the procedure illustrated by \textcite{Sesana_2010}, at a given binary separation, $K_u(a,e)$ is obtained from Equation (\ref{K fit Sesana}) by linearly interpolating in the eccentricity domain the best-fitting parameters reported in Table \ref{tab:K best-fit_parameters}. The interpolation is supplemented by the boundary conditions $K(a,e=0)=0$, as theoretically predicted in the test-particle limit for circular orbits, since circular binaries remain circular, and $K(a,e=1)=0$.\\Thus far, this Section has focused on introducing the main functions and equations that characterize the self-consistent hybrid model for the dynamical evolution of BH binaries in a fixed stellar environment. We are now in a position to illustrate how the model can be applied to an arbitrary stellar background and how the corresponding evolution of the binary separation and eccentricity, $a(t)$ and $e(t)$, is obtained numerically.\\As reported by \textcite{Quinlan_1996} and \textcite{Sesana_2006}, the three-body scattering experiments underlying the hybrid model consider stars that are initially unbound to the binary and whose velocities follow a Maxwellian distribution. Since these calculations are performed in a specific stellar background, a prescription is required to extend their results to the diverse stellar distributions hosting MBHs. A useful calibration is provided by \textcite{Sesana_2015}, who showed that the hardening rate measured from direct $N$-body simulations of a MBHB embedded in a spherically symmetric and isotropic stellar system, characterized by a mass density $\rho(r)$ and velocity dispersion $\sigma(r)$, agrees to within approximately $30\%$ with that predicted by the hybrid model for a homogeneous stellar background characterized by a constant density $\rho_{inf}$ and velocity dispersion $\sigma_{inf}$, denoting the stellar mass density and velocity dispertion values at the binary influence radius respectively. Indeed, once the binary has ejected most of the stars initially bound within its radius of influence, the loss cone is predominantly replenished by stars coming from just outside $r_{inf}$, thus having locally a nearly homogeneous spatial distribution with $\rho=\rho_{inf}$ and velocity dispersion $\sigma=\sigma_{inf}$.\\In summary, once the BH masses and the properties of the stellar background are specified, the dynamical evolution of the binary is governed by the following system of coupled differential equations:
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\frac{da}{dt} &=-\frac{a^2 G \rho_{inf}}{\sigma_{inf}}H_u(a)-\frac{64}{5} \frac{G^3 \mu M_{12}^2}{c^5 a^3} \frac{1}{(1 - e^2)^{7/2}} \left( 1 + \frac{73}{24}e^2 + \frac{37}{96}e^4 \right)    \\
\frac{de}{dt} &= -\frac{a G \rho_{inf}}{\sigma_{inf}}H_u(a)K_u(a,e) -\frac{304}{15} \frac{G^3 \mu M_{12}^2}{c^5 a^4}  \frac{e}{(1 - e^2)^{5/2}} \left( 1 + \frac{121}{304}e^2 \right)
\end{aligned}
\right.
\label{hybrid model used differential equations}
\end{equation}
which can be solved numerically for a given set of initial conditions $(a_0,e_0)$.\\The system of Equations (\ref{hybrid model used differential equations}) constitutes a set of first-order autonomous ordinary differential equations (ODEs), which can be written in the general form
\begin{equation}\label{ODEs}
    \frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{u}=(a,e)$ is the vector of dynamical variables.\\The numerical integration of this system with the adopted fourth-order Runge--Kutta (RK4) method requires the time step to satisfy an appropriate stability condition. The stability analysis to the first order of RK4 applied to a system of $N$ ODEs in the $N$-dimensional vector $\mathbf{u}$ yields the following criterion \parencite{Butcher_book}:
\begin{equation}
\label{RK4 stability criterion}
    \Delta t \le  min\left \{\frac{2.79}{|\lambda_i|}\right\} \qquad \forall i=1,N
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_i$ denotes the $i$-th eigenvalue of the Jacobian matrix $\mathbf{J}=\partial\mathbf{f}/\partial\mathbf{u}$.\\For the system considered here, the Jacobian elements can be derived directly from Equations (\ref{hybrid model used differential equations}) as
\begin{equation}\label{J_11 of Sesana comparison}
    J_{11}=\left. \frac{da}{dt}\right|_u\left(\frac{2}{a}+\frac{\gamma}{a+a_0a_h}\right)-\left.\frac{da}{dt}\right|_{gw}\left(\frac{3}{a}\right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{J_12 of Sesana comparison}
    J_{12}=\left. \frac{da}{dt}\right|_{GW}\left(\frac{{7e}}{1-e^2}+\frac{73e/12+37e^3/24}{1+73e^2/24+37e^4/96}\right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{J_21 of Sesana comparison}
    J_{21}=\left.\frac{de}{dt}\right|_u\left(\frac{1}{a}+\frac{\gamma_H}{a+a_{0,H}a_h}+\frac{\gamma_K}{a+a_{0,K}a_h}\right)-\left.\frac{de}{dt}\right|_{GW}\left(\frac{4}{a}\right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{J_22 of Sesana comparison}
    J_{22}=\left.\frac{de}{dt}\right|_u\left(\frac{\partial \ln{K}}{\partial e}\right)+\left.\frac{de}{dt}\right|_{GW}\left(\frac{5e}{1-e^2}+\frac{1+363e^2/304}{e+121e^3/304}\right).
\end{equation}
These expressions allow the two eigenvalues, $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, of the Jacobian matrix to be evaluated at each point along the binary evolutionary track and the adaptive time-step to be calculated as
\begin{equation}
    \Delta t \le 2.79 \cdot \min\left\{
    \frac{1}{|\lambda_1|};
    \frac{1}{|\lambda_2|}
    \right\}.
\end{equation}
In the following sections, \S~\ref{sec:Stellar Cusps IMBH Binary Evolution}-\ref{sec:NGC 205 IMBH Binary evolution}, will be presented the results of the numerical experiments performed with the hybrid model described above, considering different IMBH binaries masses, initial conditions and host stellar systems.

















\section{Stellar Cusps IMBH Binary Evolution}
\label{sec:Stellar Cusps IMBH Binary Evolution}
Observational evidences and direct $N$-body simulations suggest that galaxies hosting dynamically evolved BH binaries may undergo stellar scouring during binary hardening, significantly altering the original stellar distribution and leading to the formation of a shallower inner cusp (see, e.g., \textcite{Milosavljevic_2001}; \textcite{Merritt_2005}). As the binary orbit shrinks within the galactic core, it transfers energy to the surrounding stars, progressively depleting the central stellar distribution and producing a stellar mass deficit in the innermost region of the host galaxy.\\In light of these results, the first stellar background considered here for studying the dynamical evolution of an IMBH binary, from pairing to coalescence, is thus a power-law density profile within the binary influence radius, matched to a singular isothermal sphere (SIS) outside it \parencite{Sesana_2010}. The stellar density $\rho(r)$ of this spherically symmetric distribution is given by
\begin{flalign}
&
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\rho(r) &= \rho_{inf}\left(\frac{r}{r_{inf}}\right)^{-\gamma}
&\qquad \text{if} \quad r<r_{inf} \\
\rho(r) &= \frac{\sigma^2}{2\pi G r^2}
&\qquad \text{if} \quad r>r_{inf}
\end{aligned}
\right.
&&
\label{Sesana_2010 stellar cusp profile}
\end{flalign}
where $\gamma$ is the exponent of the inner power-law stellar cusp and $\sigma$ denotes the position-independent velocity dispersion of the SIS $(\gamma=2)$ profile.\\Adopting the notation introduced in the previous Section, we assume that $\sigma$ is uniquely determined by the binary mass $M_{12}$ through the $M-\sigma$ relation
\begin{equation}\label{Tremaine_2002 M_bh}
    M_6=0.84\sigma_{70}^4.
\end{equation}
provided by \textcite{Tremaine_2002}, with $\sigma_{70}$ indicating the stellar velocity dispersion in units of $70\,km/s$ and $M_6$ the binary mass in units of $10^6\,M_\odot$.\\Imposing the continuity of the stellar profile in Equation (\ref{Sesana_2010 stellar cusp profile}) at the binary influence radius $r_{inf}$ returns the following analytic expressions for $r_{inf}$ and $\rho_{inf}$:
\begin{equation}
 r_{inf}=(3-\gamma)\frac{GM_{12}}{\sigma^2},
 \label{Stellar cusps r_inf}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \rho_{inf}=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\pi G r_{inf}^2}.
 \label{stellar cusp rho_inf}
\end{equation}
As already mentioned in \S~\ref{sec:Two-Body Relaxation and Dynamical Friction}, dynamical friction exerted by the stellar background on an intruding secondary BH efficiently drags it towards the galactic center, where it eventually forms a gravitationally bound binary with the primary BH, with semimajor axis $a_0$. This separation is defined as the radius enclosing a stellar mass equal to the secondary BH mass $m_2$ and, for the adopted stellar cusp profile, can be expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{Sesana_2010 a_0}
a_0=(3-\gamma)\frac{GM_{12}}{\sigma^2}\left(\frac{q}{1+q}\right)^{1/(3-\gamma)}=r_{inf}\left(\frac{q}{1+q}\right)^{1/(3-\gamma)},
\end{equation}
In addition to $a_0$, it is useful to define a set of characteristic distances associated with different binary separations, which allows us to distinguish the various stages of binary evolution and identify the dominant physical mechanism governing each stage. Starting the binary evolution from $a_0$, the binary is initially too “soft”, in the sense of Heggie's law, for scattering with unbound stars to significantly contribute to its hardening. Therefore, once dynamical friction has become inefficient at $a_0$, the subsequent binary evolution is driven by interactions with bound stars orbiting within the binary sphere of influence \parencite{Sesana_2008}. As the binary hardens, these stars are progressively ejected from the central region, carrying away the orbital energy extracted from the binary and causing its separation to decrease towards the hardening radius
\begin{equation}
a_h=\frac{1}{4(3-\gamma)}\left(\frac{q}{1+q}\right)^{(2-\gamma)/(3-\gamma)},
\label{hardening radius sec 2.2}
\end{equation}
below which the stellar-scattering term included in Equations (\ref{hybrid model used differential equations}) becomes dominant \parencite{Sesana_2010}.\\Finally, provided that a sufficient supply of loss-cone stars is available, the binary eventually enters the GW-dominated regime, in which GW emission drives the orbital decay towards final coalescence. For a stellar cups like stellar background, the transition occurs at the binary separation $a_{GW}$ defined by the condition $H_u=H_{GW}$ \parencite{Sesana_2010}, which is
\begin{equation}
\label{cusp a_GW}
a_{GW}=4\left[\frac{128\pi(3-\gamma) F(e)}{5H_u}\right]^{1/5}\frac{\sigma}{c}q^{-4/5}(1+q)^{3/5}a_h
\end{equation}
and $F(e)$ is  a function of the eccentricity $e$ introduced in Equation (\ref{F(e) definition}).\\In conclusion, for the equal-mass BH binaries considered in this work, the initial binary separation $a_0$, given by Equation (\ref{Sesana_2010 a_0}), and the hardening radius $a_h$, defined in Equation (\ref{hardening radius sec 2.2}), are comparable. In particular, their separation in logarithmic space is small compared with the overall range of binary separations spanned during the subsequent evolution. Consequently, the binary is already close to the hard-binary regime at its birth, and the additional hardening associated with the erosion of the bound stellar cusp can safely be omitted from our analysis \parencite{Sesana_2015}.\\To investigate the role played by the central stellar density in the overall evolution of the binary, while keeping all other relevant conditions fixed, we consider three numerical experiments, one for each value of the stellar cusp slope $\gamma=(1,1.5,2)$. Using the hybrid model described above, we follow the evolutionary tracks of the same equal-mass IMBH binary, with $m_1=m_2=10^5\,M_\odot$, initially placed at the same initial conditions $(a_0,e_0)$ in all cases, but embedded in stellar backgrounds characterized by different cusp slopes. Although the value of $a_0$ depends on the adopted cusp slope $\gamma$, as determined from Equation (\ref{Sesana_2010 a_0}), we adopt a common initial configuration for all binaries, $(\bar{a}_0,0.5)$, where $\bar{a}_0$ is the average of the $a_0$ values obtained for the different stellar cusps. All stellar cusps are instead characterized by the same velocity dispersion, $\sigma=48.9,\mathrm{km/s}$, owing to Equation (\ref{Tremaine_2002 M_bh}). Combined with the different cusp slopes, this common value of $\sigma$ yields the corresponding stellar densities at the influence radius, $\rho_{inf}=(1.71,3.04,6.83)\times10^5 \,M_\odot/pc^3$ for $\gamma=(1,1.5,2)$, respectively.\\Following the prescriptions given by Equations (\ref{Stellar cusps r_inf}), (\ref{Sesana_2010 a_0}), (\ref{hardening radius sec 2.2}), and (\ref{cusp a_GW}), the characteristic transition lengthscales are computed for each stellar background, or equivalently for each value of $\gamma$, and reported in Table \ref{tab:cusps lengthscales}.

Having reported how the essential ingredients of the adopted hybrid model are have been derived, we can now specify the setup adopted for the numerical integrations. In all the experiments presented in this Section, the integration starts from the initial conditions $(\bar{a}_0,e_0)=(0.34,\mathrm{pc},0.5)$ and is terminated when the binary separation reaches the Schwarzschild radius of the binary, defined as
\begin{equation}
R_s=\frac{2GM_{12}}{c^2},
\end{equation}
which, for the total binary mass considered here, corresponds to $R_s=5.9\times10^5\, km$. Moreover, the integration time step $\Delta t$ is chosen to ensure the numerical stability of the RK4 scheme according to the criterion given in Equation (\ref{RK4 stability criterion}). For all numerical runs, we adopt
\begin{equation}

\section{NGC 205 IMBH Binary evolution}
\label{sec:NGC 205 IMBH Binary evolution}

The Local Group comprises several dozen galaxies, with the majority of its members being low-mass systems gravitationally associated with its two dominant galaxies, the Milky Way and M31 \parencite{Cimatti_book}. Among them, M 32, NGC 205, NGC 5102, and NGC 5206, four nucleated low-mass early-type galaxies, are of particular interest because their central regions can be resolved in detail and their nuclear dynamics can be studied through high-resolution observations \parencite{Nguyen_2018}. In this Section, we investigate the evolution of an IMBH binary embedded in NGC 205, with particular emphasis on the role played by the binary eccentrecy $e_0$ at pairing on the resulting evolutionary tracks and coalescence timescales.\\NGC 205 is a nucleated dwarf elliptical galaxy orbiting M 31, located at $\mathrm{RA}=00^{\mathrm h}40^{\mathrm m}22.1^{\mathrm s}$ and $\mathrm{Dec}=+41^\circ41'07''$. Its distance has been determined through the tip of the red giant branch method, yielding a distance modulus of $(m-M)_0=24.58\pm0.07$, corresponding to a distance of approximately $824\pm27$~kpc \parencite{McConnachie_2005}. HST/STIS spectroscopy of the Ca II triplet region revealed no significant nuclear rotation and a nearly constant velocity dispersion of $\sim20~\mathrm{km,s^{-1}}$ within the central $\sim0.3''$ ($\sim1$~pc). Dynamical modelling of the nuclear region yielded a $3\sigma$ upper limit of $3.8\times10^4,M_\odot$ for the mass of a possible central IMBH (see, e.g., \textcite{Valluri_2005}; \textcite{Geha_2006}).\\Following \textcite{Khan_2021}, we adopt NGC 205 as a representative present-day, nucleated dwarf galaxy and use its observed properties to set up numerical experiments with the Quinlan--Sesana hybrid model. Its two-dimensional surface-brightness profile can be decomposed into two distinct structural components, corresponding to the bulge and the nuclear star cluster. We model each component with a three-dimensional deprojected Sersic density profile of the form (see, e.g., \textcite{Prugniel_1997}; \textcite{Terzic_2005}):
\begin{equation}\label{Prugniel Sersic profile}
    \rho(r)=\rho_0\left(\frac{r}{R_e}\right)^{-p}e^{-b\left(\frac{r}{R_e}\right)^{1/n}}.
\end{equation}
The stellar density profile given in Equation (\ref{Prugniel Sersic profile}) is characterized by three structural parameters, $(\rho_0,R_e,n)$, together with the model-dependent quantities $p(n)$ and $b(n)$. Here, $R_e$ is the effective radius, enclosing half of the total luminosity of the stellar component, while $n$ is the Sersic index controlling the shape of the profile. The normalization $\rho_0$ is determined by requiring the volume-integrated density to reproduce the total stellar mass of the component. The parameters $p$ and $b$ are functions of the Sersic index and can be approximated, for $0.5<n<10$, as
$p=1.0-0.6097/n+0.05563/n^2$ and $b \approx 2n-1/3+0.009876/n$ \parencite{Terzic_2005}.\\Table \ref{tab:NGC 205 structural parameters} summarizes the main structural parameters of the bulge and NSC of NGC 205, which are required to specify the corresponding Sersic profiles.

Since Chapter~\ref{chap:chapter3} is devoted to studying the dynamical evolution of IMBH binaries embedded in Sersic-like stellar backgrounds through direct $N$-body simulations, we briefly examine here the properties of this profile, defined by Equation (\ref{Prugniel Sersic profile}), that are relevant for constructing equilibrium $N$-body initial conditions and interpreting the results presented in the following chapter.\\Given the mass density profile in Equation (\ref{Prugniel Sersic profile}) for a spherically symmetric stellar component of total mass $M_*$, the mass enclosed within a sphere of radius $r$ is obtained by integrating the density profile over the corresponding volume:
\begin{equation}
M(<r)=4\pi\int_0^r dx\, \rho(x)x^2.
\end{equation}
By exploiting the standard substitution $Z\equiv b\left(\frac{r}{R_e}\right)^{1/n}$, the integral can be expressed in terms of the lower incomplete gamma function,
\begin{equation}\label{lower incomplete gamma fun}
    \gamma(a,x)=\int_{0}^{x}dt\,e^{-t}t^{a-1},
\end{equation}
as
\begin{equation}\label{Sersic mass profile with rho_0}
M(<r)=4\pi \rho_0 R_e^3 n b^{n(p-3)}\gamma\left[n(3-p),b\left(\frac{r}{R_e}\right)^ {1/n}\right].
\end{equation}
Imposing the normalization condition $M(<r)\rightarrow M_*$ as $r\rightarrow+\infty$, and using the fact that the lower incomplete gamma function reduces to the complete gamma function in this limit,
\begin{equation}\label{complete gamma fun}
    \Gamma(a)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\, e^{-t}t^{a-1},
\end{equation}
Equation (\ref{Sersic mass profile with rho_0}) can be conveniently recast in terms of the total stellar mass as
\begin{equation}\label{Sersic mass profile with M_*}
M(<r)=M_*\frac{\gamma\left[n(3-p),b\left(\frac{r}{R_e}\right)^ {1/n}\right]}{\Gamma[n(3-p)]}
\end{equation}
and the normalizing density $\rho_0$ is unambiguously set by comparing Equations (\ref{Sersic mass profile with rho_0}) and (\ref{Sersic mass profile with M_*}) to
\begin{equation}
\label{Sersic profile rho_0}
\rho_0
=
\frac{M_*}{4\pi R_e^3}
\frac{b^{\,n(3-p)}}{n\,\Gamma\!\left[n(3-p)\right]}.
\end{equation}
Lastly, the gravitational potential can therefore be written in the general form
\begin{equation}\label{spherical potential, general formula}
\Phi(r)=\Phi(r_0)+\frac{GM(<r_0)}{r_0}-\frac{GM(<r)}{r}-4\pi G \int_{r}^{r_0}dx\,\rho(x)x.
\end{equation}
Although the stellar distribution is formally untruncated, its enclosed mass converges to the finite value $M_*$ as $r\rightarrow+\infty$, allowing for the standard normalization $\Phi(\infty)=0$ and $r_0\rightarrow+\infty$. In this way, Equation (\ref{spherical potential, general formula}) reduces to
\begin{equation}\label{spherical potential, simplified formula}
\Phi(r)=-\frac{GM(<r)}{r}-4\pi G \int_{r}^{\infty}dx\,\rho(x)x.
\end{equation}
Substituting the Prugniel--Sérsic density profile reported in Equation (\ref{Prugniel Sersic profile}) into Equation (\ref{spherical potential, simplified formula}), the integral can be evaluated in terms of the upper incomplete gamma function,
\begin{equation}\label{upper incomplete gamma fun}
\Gamma(a,x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}dt,e^{-t}t^{a-1},
\end{equation}
yielding
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(r)=-\frac{GM_*}{r}
    \left\{
        \frac{\gamma\left[n(3-p),Z\right]}{\Gamma\left[n(3-p)\right]}
        +Z^n\frac{\Gamma\left[n(2-p),Z\right]}{\Gamma\left[n(3-p)\right]}
    \right\}.
\end{equation}
We can now turn to the suite of numerical experiments performed. Here, we present the evolutionary tracks obtained by evolving equal-mass IMBH binaries, with component masses $m_1=m_2=4\times 10^4,M_\odot$, within the stellar environment of NGC 205 \parencite{Khan_2021}. It requires only the additional computation of the stellar mass density $\rho_{inf}$ and velocity dispersion $\sigma_{inf}$ at the binary influence radius $r_{inf}$, both set by the IMBH binary and stellar environment featues.\\Therefore, using Equation (\ref{Sersic profile rho_0}), the normalizing densities of the NGC 205 stellar bulge and NSC are found to be $\rho_{0,Bulge}=2.95\,M_\odot/pc^r$ and $\rho_{0,NSC}=4.80\times 10^5\,M_\odot/pc^3$, respectively. The 125 runs performed to explore the redshift and initial eccentricity parameter spaces provide, in addition to an equal number of pairs of evolutionary tracks $(t,a(t))$ and $(t,e(t))$, an estimate of the coalescence time $t_{coal}$ for each sampled configuration. Here, $t_{coal}$ is defined as the time elapsed from the beginning of the numerical integration, corresponding to the initial binary conditions $(a_0,e_0)$, to the IMBH binary coalescence, assumed to occur at $a=R_s$. Whether a given galaxy model and set of binary initial conditions can host a GW-loud source detectable by LISA or ET in the present-day Universe depends not only on the estimated $t_{coal}$, but also on the cosmic time $t(z)$ corresponding to the redshift of the galaxy model (see, e.g. \textcite{Coles_book}).\\In standard $\Lambda$CDM  cosmology, within which the Friedmann--Lemaître--Robertson--Walker (FLRW) metric provides the appropriate description of the large-scale homogeneous and isotropic Universe, the Hubble function $H(z)$ describes the expansion rate of the Universe as a function of redshift and can be written as
\begin{equation}
    H(z) = H_0 \sqrt{\Omega_{k}(1+z)^2\Omega_{m}(1+z)^3 + \Omega_{r}(1+z)^4 +  + \Omega_{\Lambda}},
    \label{Hubble function}
\end{equation}
where $H_0$ is the present-day Hubble constant, $\Omega_{k}$, $\Omega_{m}$, $\Omega_{r}$, and $\Omega_{\Lambda}$ are the present-day dimensionless density parameters associated with matter, radiation, spatial curvature, and dark energy, respectively. In the FLRW cosmological model, the cosmic time $t(z)$ corresponding to a given redshift $z$ can be obtained from the Hubble function through the standard redshift--time relation
\begin{equation}
    t(z) = \int_z^\infty \frac{dz'}{(1+z')H(z')}.
    \label{cosmic time redshift}
\end{equation}
Adopting the cosmological parameters provided by \textcite{PLANK_2020}, namely $H_0=67.4\,km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}$ and $\Omega_{m}=0.315$, and assuming a geometrically flat $\Lambda$CDM Universe, the cosmic time $t(z)$ is determined for each galaxy model from Equation (\ref{cosmic time redshift}). In this framework, $\Omega_{\Lambda}=1-\Omega_{m}=0.685$, the radiation contribution is negligible at the redshifts considered and the age of the universe is estimated to be $t_{age}=t(z=0)=13.8\,Gyr$. This allows the coalescence times obtained from the numerical integrations, measured from the initial epoch of each model, to be converted into the corresponding cosmic times at which the IMBH binaries reach coalescence. In this chapter, we have investigated the self-consistent hybrid model developed by \textcite{Quinlan_1996} and \textcite{Sesana_2006} to describe the dynamical evolution of massive black hole binaries within a fixed stellar background. Taking advantage of the versatility of this framework, we have performed a suite of numerical experiments aimed at identifying the main features of IMBH binary evolutionary tracks and systematically exploring the relevant parameter space. The collection of experiments presented in \S~\ref{sec:Stellar Cusps IMBH Binary Evolution}-\ref{sec:NGC 205 IMBH Binary evolution}-\ref{sec:Cosmic Size Evolution Down to NGC 205}, together with the analysis of the corresponding evolutionary tracks $(t,a(t))$ and $(t,e(t))$, allows us to draw the following main conclusions:

\begin{itemize}
\item Stellar environments characterised by enhanced central densities, such as nucleated galaxies, promote more efficient dynamical hardening of IMBH binaries, leading to a faster orbital decay.
\item Eccentric IMBH binaries generally undergo further eccentricity growth during the stellar-scattering hardening phase. As a consequence, they can enter the GW-dominated regime at bigger eccentricity and reach coalescence on shorter timescales.

\item Initially circular binaries tend to preserve their low eccentricity throughout both  stellar-driven and GW-driven regimes.

\item In sufficiently low-density stellar environments, the stellar-driven hardening can be too inefficient to bring the binary into the GW-dominated regime within a Hubble time $t_H$, potentially preventing its coalescence within the present time and detection by LISA or ET.

\item Present-day, non-nucleated dwarf galaxies characterised by low central densities may have been sufficiently compact at higher redshifts, $z>0$, to enable an IMBH binary hosted by them to coalesce within the present-day universe.

\end{itemize}
The results obtained in this chapter motivate the next step of our investigation, namely the transition from the semi-analytical hybrid description to direct $N$-body simulations. This approach will allow us to follow the evolution of the IMBH binary and its surrounding stellar system self-consistently, overcoming some of the main limitations inherent to the adopted hybrid model. In particular, direct $N$-body simulations will make it possible to follow the mutual dynamical evolution of the binary and the stellar background, rather than prescribing the latter as a fixed external potential, and will open the possibility of investigating the dynamical evolution and eventual merger of the stellar systems themselves.

\section{Dynamical Equilibrium of Stellar Systems}
\label{sec:Dynamical Equilibrium of Stellar Systems}
The first step towards performing $N$-body experiments of merging stellar systems hosting IMBHs is the generation of reliable $N$-body initial conditions that capture the main properties of the system in isolation, when at equilibrium, and reproduce its relevant observables, such as the density profile and stellar velocity dispersion, consistently with those of the corresponding idealized model. This, in turn, requires a well-defined framework for describing the dynamical state of the system.\\As discussed in \S~\ref{sec:Two-Body Relaxation and Dynamical Friction}, provided that the stellar system is considered on timescales shorter than its two-body relaxation time $t_{2b}$, the discrete nature of the stellar component can be neglected: collisional effects are negligible, and the stars can be treated as collisionless particles moving in a smooth gravitational potential generated by a continuous mass density distribution $\rho$, smooth proxy of the actual stellar system. In this regime, when dealing with stars that are all identical to one another, the dynamical state of the stellar component can be described by a unique one-particle distribution function (DF) $f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)$ of phase space (PhS). Therefore, within $t_{2b}$, the system is said to be at equilibrium, its DF is time-independent and satisfies the Collisionless Boltzmann Equation (\ref{CBE}), while the corresponding gravitational potential $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$ is self-consistently determined by the mass distribution $\rho(\mathbf{x})$ through the Poisson equation
\begin{equation}
\label{Poisson equation}
 \nabla^2 \Phi(\mathbf{x})=4\pi G\rho(\mathbf{x}).
\end{equation}
Let us now examine in greater detail the properties of the DF, the CBE, and the description of equilibrium states in $N$-body systems, building on the discussion  in \S~\ref{sec:Two-Body Relaxation and Dynamical Friction}.\\We begin by introducing the normalized DF in phase space. Denoting by $\mathbf{w}$ an arbitrary set of canonical coordinates in phase space, we adopt the normalization
\begin{equation}\label{DF normalized}
\int_{\mathbb{R}^6}d\mathbf{w}, f(\mathbf{w})=1.
\end{equation}
The CBE follows from requiring the DF to be conserved along the trajectories generated by the potential $\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)$ associated with the density distribution $\rho(\mathbf{x},t)$. Taking the total derivative of the DF along these trajectories gives
\begin{equation}\label{CBE new}
    \frac{Df}{Dt}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+
    \mathbf{\dot{w}}\cdot \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{w}}=
    \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+[f,H]=0,
\end{equation}
where $D/Dt$ denotes the Lagrangian derivative,
the square bracket indicates the Poisson bracket
\begin{equation}
[f,H]
=
\sum_i
\left(
\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}
\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}
-
\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}
\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}
\right),
\label{Poisson bracket}
\end{equation}
and $H$ is the Hamiltonian function of the system (see, e.g. \citealt{Binney_book}).\\The arbitrariness of the canonical coordinates $\mathbf{w}$ enables us to specialize Equation (\ref{CBE}) to the inertial Cartesian coordinates $(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})$. With this choice, the Hamiltonian becomes
\begin{equation}\label{Hamiltonian in cartesian coord.}
    H(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)=\frac{1}{2}v^2+\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)
\end{equation}
and the Equation (\ref{CBE new}) turns into
\begin{equation}\label{CBE in cartesian coord.}
    \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\mathbf{v}\cdot
    \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{x}}-
    \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \mathbf{x}}
    \cdot \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{v}}=0.
\end{equation}
Being interested in studying the equilibrium condition of galaxies and in particular of spherically symmetric models, it is usefull the following definition: a dynamical system is said to be stationary if $\partial f/\partial t=0$. Therefore, collisionless systems that have achieved equilibrium will be provided of a DF obeying to the steady-state CBE
\begin{equation}\label{Stationary CBE}
    \frac{Df}{Dt}=
    \mathbf{\dot{w}}\cdot \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{w}}=
    [f,H]=0.
\end{equation}
Solutions to Equation (\ref{Stationary CBE}) are referred to as integrals of motion. Specifically, the function $I(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})$ is an integral of motion for the potential $\Phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})$ if it satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{Integral of motion def}
    \frac{D}{Dt}[I(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{v}(t))]=0
\end{equation}
along any orbit of the potential and a relevant example is represented by the Hamiltonian itself of the system, provided
\begin{equation}
 [H,H]=0.
\end{equation}
The comparison between Equation Equation (\ref{Stationary CBE}) and (\ref{Integral of motion def}) reveals that solving the steady-state CBE is equivalent to being an integral of motion; this leads to the formulation of the Jeans theorem.
\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Jeans Theorem. --}
\emph{Any steady-state solution of the collisionless Boltzmann equation depends on
the phase-space coordinates only through integrals of motion in the given potential, and any function of the integrals yields a steady-state solution of the collisionless Boltzmann
equation.}
\medskip

\noindent The remaining quantities to be defined, which correspond to observables that can be inferred from kinematic measurements, are the first- and second-order velocity moments of the distribution function. These are

\begin{equation}\label{mean velocity of DF}
    \overline{v_i}(\mathbf{x},t)\equiv 
    \frac{1}{\rho(\mathbf{x},t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^6} d^3 \mathbf{v}\,f v_i
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{velocity-dispersion of DF}
    \sigma_{ij}^2(\mathbf{x},t)\equiv 
    \frac{1}{\rho(\mathbf{x},t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^6} d^3 \mathbf{v}\,
    f (v_i-\overline{v_i})(v_j-\overline{v_j})=\overline{v_iv_j}-
    \overline{v_i}\,\overline{v_j},
\end{equation}
respectively the mean velocity and the velocity dispersion tensor.\\We are now finally in a position to recall the most peculiar kinematical properties of an important subclass of equilibrium models, namely Ergodic systems. The Jeans Theorem imposes that the DF $f$ of systems at equilibrium will be any non-negative function of $H$; in case $f$ depends on $H$ only, i.e. $f=f(H)$, then systems with this type of DF are said Ergodic.\\Let us focus on first-order moment of the DF. In the inertial Cartesian frame where stars move under the action of a steady-state potential $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$, the Hamiltonian of the system is an even function of $\mathbf{v}$, causing $\overline{v_x}(\mathbf{x})=\overline{v_y}(\mathbf{x})=\overline{v_z}(\mathbf{x})$ to vanish.\\Similarly, it can be demonstrated that the velocity-dispersion tensor is isotropic; thus it holds
\begin{equation}\label{Ergodic velocity dispersion tensor}
 \sigma^2_{ij}(\mathbf{x})=\sigma^2(\mathbf{x})\delta_{ij}=\delta_{ij}\frac{4\pi}{3\rho(\mathbf{x})}\int_0^{+\infty}dv\,v^4f\left(\frac{1}{2}v^2+\Phi(\mathbf{x})\right)
\end{equation}
Therefore, every system characterized by an ergodic DF is isotropic. In the next Section, the properties of the generated steady-state isotropic stellar distributions will be presented and compared with the theoretical predictions summarized above.\\So far, we have been concerned with the velocity-moments of the distribution function of Ergodic systems; however, the generation of initial conditions (ICs) for N-body, equilibrium models relies on the correct sampling of the DF itself. Recovering it from $\rho$ for globally isotropic stellar systems can be done by means of the Eddington's formula
\begin{equation}\label{Eddington's formula}
f(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{8}\pi^2}\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}
\int_{\varepsilon_t}^{\varepsilon}
\frac{d\rho}{d\Psi}\frac{d\Psi}{\sqrt{\varepsilon-\Psi}}
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon=-H$ and $\Psi=-\Phi$ being the relative energy and potential respectively
and $\varepsilon_t$ the truncation energy of the system.\\Finally, the last tool introduced in this Section is the virial ratio, closely connected with the Virial theorem, which provides a fast diagnostic for determining whether the generated model is in equilibrium or is either evolving toward collapse or expansion. By definition a self-gravitating system, constituted by N particles, with total kinteic energy
\begin{equation}
K = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{1}{2} m_i v_i^2
\end{equation}
and gravitational potential energy
\begin{equation}
U = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j \ne i} \frac{Gm_i m_j}{|\mathbf{x_i}-\mathbf{x_j}|}
\end{equation}
is virialized if it holds
\begin{equation}
   2K'+U=0 \qquad \forall t
\end{equation}
in the barycentric inertial reference system $S_{CM}$. For these systems the virial ratio
\begin{equation}\label{virial ratio}
    Q\equiv\frac{2K'}{|U|}
\end{equation}
should be close to unity. In presence of a non vanishing velocity of the system center of mass, which flags that the time evolution is studied in a frame $S$ moving with respet to $S_{CM}$, Equation (\ref{virial ratio}) must be adjusted as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{virial ratio without offset}
    Q\equiv\frac{2(K-K_{CM})}{|U|}.
\end{equation}



\section{OpOpGadget and Galaxy Modeling}
Once recasted the main aspects of the theory underlying the dynamical equilibrium configuration of stellar systems, with focus on Ergodic DFs, we can now address the problem of generatic N-body ICs for stellar populations which are compatible with the inferred quantites: total mass, density profile and associated structural parameters.\\Therefore, stellar positions and velocities are generated by means of \href{https://github.com/giulianoiorio/OpOpGadget}{\texttt{OpOpGadget}}. This Python module offers a number of tools for both generating N-body ICs and analyzing  N-body simulations outputs. OpOpGadget is equipped with an extensive library of analytic models for spherically symmetric stellar distributions. The first step in the workflow initialization is to specify the desired model, assign the values of the parameters appearing in its functional form, and choose the number of particles to represent the stellar environment. Then, particles spherical coordinates are assigned by randomly picked the cumulative mass profile
\begin{equation}\label{cumulative mass formula}
 M_*(<r)=4\pi\int_0^{r}dy\,y^2\rho(y)
\end{equation}
through the variable $u=np.random.uniform(M_{min}=0, M_{max}=M_*, N)$. Because $M(<r)$ is a monotonically increasing function of radius, it can be inverted so that $N$ radii of kind $r=M_*^{-1}(u)$ are finally obtained. Yet, two additional independent random variables, uniformly distributed in the intervals $[-1,1]$ and $[0,2\pi]$ respectively, are used to sample the direction cosine and the azimuthal angle, thereby obtaining $N$ sets of spherical coordinates $(r,\theta,\phi.)$, which are subsequently transformed into Cartesian coordinates.\\While particle positions are drawn from the cumulative mass distribution, the velocity components are obtained by sampling the DF, assuming that the gravitational potential $\Phi$ is generated by the mass distribution associated with the previously initialized particles. Three random numbers $\mathbf{v}$ lying in the range $[-1,1]$ allows to temporary assign a normalized velocity to each particle, which will found to have the binding energy $\varepsilon=\Psi(1-v^2)$. Finally, the Von Neumann rejection method is implemented and the normalized velocity is accepted if
\begin{equation}\label{Von Neumann rejection criterion}
 u\le \frac{f(\varepsilon)}{f(\varepsilon=\Psi)},
\end{equation}
where $u$ is another indipendent random number in $[0,1]$. If the Inequality (\ref{Von Neumann rejection criterion}) is true, the final velocity vector will be obtained by taking the product between the normalized one and the factor $\sqrt{2\Psi}$. At the end of the process, each particle will have its own $ID$, mass $m=M_*/N$, position $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{v}$.\\Let us denote with $p$ the variable containing all this information, OpOpGadget provides already-implemented methods to this objects and the most relevant are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{\emph{NbodyModel:}} generates a spherically symmetric, multi-component, equilibrium model as mentioned above
 \item \textbf{\emph{Analysis(NbodyModel):}}  takes as argument an $NbodyModel$ object and is furnished with the following methods, used in our analysis:
 \begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{\emph{Analysis.com():}} returns Cartesian and velocity of $NbodyModel$
 \item \textbf{\emph{Analysis.softening\_scale(kernel=Gadget):}} calculates the softening scale $\epsilon$ using the spline kernel of Gadget2 \parencite{Springel_2005}. This softening replaces the Dirac delta function in $\rho(r)=m\delta(r)$ with the normalized spline kernel
\begin{equation}\label{Spline Kernel}
W(r,h)=
\begin{cases}
1-6q^2+6q^3 & \text{if } 0\le q<1/2 \\
2(1-q)^3 & \text{if } 1/2\le q<1, \\
0 & \text{if } q\ge 1.
\end{cases}  \qquad  \qquad q\equiv \frac{r}{h},
\end{equation}
having  a compact support which extends to $h=2.8\epsilon$, beyond which the gravitational potential reverts to the standard Newtonian form.
  \item \textbf{\emph{Analysis.tdyn(mq=50):}} provides the dynamical time of $NbodyModel$ as $T_{dyn}=0.5\pi\sqrt{R_h^3/(GM_h)}$,
        where $R_h$ is the radius that contains the fraction $M_h=M_*\cdot mq\%$.\\This estimate has been corrected to make it compatible with the adopted definition of dynamical time $t_{dyn}$ at half-mass radius $r_{half}$, which is
\begin{equation}\label{dynamical time def}
    t_{dyn}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{G\bar{\rho}}}
\end{equation}
with $\bar{\rho}$ being, for a spherical system,
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\rho}=\frac{M/2}{\frac{4}{3}\pi r_{half}^3}.
\end{equation}
 \end{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{\emph{Profile(NbodyModel,\,xmin,\,xmax):}} has two methods that allows to reproduce between $xmin$ and $xmax$ the radial profile of relevant quantities:
 \begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{\emph{Profile.dens():}}  outputs both the radial and density grids
  \item \textbf{\emph{Profile.mass():}} outputs both the radial and cumulative mass grids
 \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}
The dwarf galaxies, along with their nuclear star clusters, that have been generated are the same as those studied within the \textcite{Sesana_2010} hybrid model framework. The comparison between the results of this semi-analytical, shown in Section (?), and the numerical merger simulations is intended to assess whether a model based on an already-formed binary black hole embedded in a single galaxy can effectively capture the properties that, in our case, emerge self-consistently from the full merger process.\\In \textcite{Khan_2021}, galaxies bulge and NSCs density profiles are expressed in terms of a Sersic profile with de-projection formula analyzed by \textcite{Prugniel} and reported in Equation (\ref{Prugniel Sersic profile}). Nevertheless, the Sersic analytic model implemented in OpOpGadget takes as a reference \textcite{Neto_1999} de-projection
\begin{equation}\label{Sersic profile Neto}
    \rho(r)=\rho_0\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^{-p}e^{-(r/a)^\nu},
\end{equation}
where $\nu=n^{-1}$, the inverse of the Sersic index, and $a$, denoting the Sersic profile scalelength at which the surface density $\Sigma(a)=\Sigma_0$, are known as primary parameters. Therefore, it is evident that the same density distribution can be described through different combinations of indipendent parameters and a proper conversion between primary and secondary, i.e. depending on primary ones, is required before continuing.Here, to pass from $a$ to $R_{eff}$, we need to use the following analytical approximation
\begin{equation}\label{Conversion between a and Reff}
    \ln{R_{eff}/a}=\frac{0.6950-\ln{\nu}}{\nu}-0.1789.
\end{equation}
Moreover, particular attention should be paid to the fact that the stellar particles to be initialized are required to be in dynamical equilibrium within the combined gravitational potential of both stars and central black hole. The presence of such a massive and compact object is expected to modify the equilibrium configuration of the stars with respect to the isolated case, primarily by increasing their velocity dispersion; this can be done by passing a Python list of OpOpGadget analytic models to NbdoyModel. Despite being a single object rather than a particles distribution, a given model can be realized even with just one particle and the adopted one for generating BHs is the Plummer sphere. According to this choice, density and cumulative mass are respectively
\begin{equation}\label{Plummer density}
\rho(r)=\frac{3M}{4\pi a^3}
\left(1+\frac{r^2}{a^2}\right)^{-5/2}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{Plummer cumulative mass}
M(<r)
=
M\frac{r^3}{\left(r^2+a^2\right)^{3/2}};
\end{equation}
here, $a$ is the Plummer scalelength and is linked to the half-mass radius $r_{1/2}$ of the distribution by
\begin{equation}\label{Plummer a to r_1/2 conversion}
a
\simeq
0.766\,r_{1/2}.
\end{equation}
The use of gravitational softening in N-body experiments sets an intrinsic spatial resolution scale, below which the system cannot be accurately resolved. Accordingly, the Plummer scale radius a is taken to be the mean interparticle distance at the half-mass radius $r_h$
\begin{equation}\label{mean intraparticle distance}
 \lambda=r_h\left(\frac{2}{N}\right)^{1/3},
\end{equation}
which serves as a proxy for the gravitational softening length of the galaxy prior to its generation. Finally, Equation (\ref{spherical potential, general formula}) shows that the Plummer potential
\begin{equation}\label{Plummer gravitational potential}
\Phi(r)
=
-\frac{GM}{\sqrt{r^2+a^2}}
\end{equation}
reduces to the Keplerian form at distances much larger than the scale radius, $r\gg a$. This asymptotic behavior explains why the Plummer model is a convenient description of BHs.
